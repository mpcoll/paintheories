## Overview

Vulvodynia is a common and highly impactful genital pain condition [@pukall2016]. In two US population samples, 7 to 8% of women reported pain consistent with vulvodynia by age 40; many never sought treatment, and more than half of those who did received no diagnosis [@harlow2014].

## How it is classified

The 2015 consensus terminology defines vulvodynia as vulvar pain of at least 3 months without a clear identifiable cause and describes it by location, provocation, onset and time course [@bornstein2016]. ICD-11 gives vulvodynia its own code (GA34.02) and lists it under chronic primary visceral pain (MG30.00), a subtype of chronic primary pain [@nicholas2019].

## Mechanisms

No single mechanism explains vulvodynia; it probably represents the end point of different factors that vary from patient to patient [@pukall2016]. Peripheral changes have been found, including more intraepithelial nerve endings in the vestibular mucosa of women with provoked pain [@bohmstarke1998]. Central changes have been found too: painful pressure on the vestibule produced stronger insular and frontal activation than in controls, a pattern described in fibromyalgia and irritable bowel syndrome [@pukall2005]. In a population-based sample, women with vulvodynia were more likely to have fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome or interstitial cystitis [@reed2012].

## Open questions

- Which subgroups exist and whether localised and generalised vulvodynia share mechanisms; longitudinal studies are lacking [@pukall2016].
- Whether peripheral nerve changes in the vestibule are a cause or a consequence of persistent pain, given that the evidence is cross-sectional [@bohmstarke1998].
