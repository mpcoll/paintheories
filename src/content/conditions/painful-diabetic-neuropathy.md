## Overview

Neuropathy is the most prevalent complication of diabetes, and distal symmetric polyneuropathy, a loss of sensory function that begins in the feet, is a very common form of it; at least half of people with diabetes develop it over time [@feldman2019]. In some of them it is painful. In a community study of more than 15,000 people with diabetes in England, about a third reported painful neuropathic symptoms and about a fifth met criteria for painful diabetic neuropathy; it was more common in type 2 diabetes, in women and in people of South Asian origin [@abbott2011].

## How it is classified

In ICD-11 the pain is coded as chronic peripheral neuropathic pain (MG30.51), with diabetic polyneuropathy (8C03.0) coded as its cause [@scholz2019; @treede2019]. The Toronto consensus defines painful diabetic neuropathy as pain that arises directly from abnormalities of the peripheral somatosensory system in diabetes, and grades the underlying neuropathy by certainty [@tesfaye2010].

## Mechanisms

Why some people with diabetic neuropathy have pain and others do not is unresolved. In a detailed phenotyping study, pain went with more severe neuropathy and higher HbA1c, and people with moderate or severe pain had more marked sensory loss, not less; an "irritable nociceptor" profile fitted only about 6% of those with neuropathic pain [@themistocleous2016]. Central factors also seem to matter: people whose conditioned pain modulation was less efficient benefited more from duloxetine, a drug thought to strengthen descending inhibition [@yarnitsky2012].

## Open questions

- What distinguishes painful from painless neuropathy of similar severity is still unclear, and no single peripheral or central marker separates them.
- Whether sensory phenotypes (for example sensory loss versus preserved hyperexcitable fibres) can guide treatment choice remains to be shown in trials.
