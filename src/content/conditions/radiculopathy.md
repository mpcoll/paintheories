## Overview

Sciatica is pain in the distribution of the sciatic nerve, most often caused by herniation of a lumbar intervertebral disc; its lifetime incidence has been estimated at 13% to 40%, and most cases resolve with simple measures [@stafford2007]. Estimates of how common it is vary widely because studies define sciatica in very different ways [@konstantinou2008]. Painful radiculopathy can involve cervical, thoracic, lumbar or sacral roots, so the pain may run down an arm as well as a leg [@scholz2019].

## How it is classified

The terms are used loosely. Radicular pain is pain evoked from a spinal nerve root, typically shooting or band-like along the limb; radiculopathy strictly means loss of root function (numbness, weakness, reduced reflexes); and somatic referred pain is pain felt in the limb but arising from spinal structures [@bogduk2009]. In ICD-11, chronic painful radiculopathy is a form of chronic peripheral neuropathic pain (MG30.51), and any accompanying back or neck pain is classified separately as musculoskeletal pain [@scholz2019; @treede2019].

## Mechanisms

Compression of the root is only part of the story: material from a herniated nucleus pulposus probably provokes a cytokine-mediated inflammatory response in the lumbar and sacral roots, and an abnormal immune response and mechanical factors may also contribute [@stafford2007]. Quantitative sensory testing shows loss of mainly large-fibre sensation in the affected dermatomes, supporting a neuropathic component; similar but milder loss in patients whose leg pain does not follow a dermatome suggests a continuum rather than two separate entities [@freynhagen2008].

## Open questions

- Telling neuropathic radicular pain from nociceptive referred leg pain at the bedside is difficult, and the two often coexist, which complicates both trials and treatment choice [@fourre2022].
- Why a minority of people with sciatica develop chronic pain while most recover is not well explained.
