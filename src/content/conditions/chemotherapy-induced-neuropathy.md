## Overview

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a common and disabling consequence of neurotoxic cancer treatment. Severe acute CIPN can force chemotherapy to be reduced or stopped. A meta-analysis found CIPN in 68% of patients in the first month after chemotherapy and in 30% at 6 months or later; prevalence differed between drugs [@seretny2014]. As cancer survival improves, more people live with its long-term effects [@colvin2019].

## How it is classified

ICD-11 places chronic painful CIPN under chronic cancer-related pain, as a form of chronic post cancer treatment pain (MG30.11), and asks for the cancer to be coded as well [@bennett2019]. The IASP neuropathic pain classification also describes it as one form of painful polyneuropathy, noting that pain develops in only a subgroup of patients with the neuropathy [@scholz2019].

## Mechanisms

The mechanisms are complex and include changes in sodium, potassium and calcium channels and transient receptor potential channels, mitochondrial dysfunction and interactions with immune cells [@colvin2019]. Clinical risk factors reported in some studies include pre-existing neuropathy, smoking, reduced creatinine clearance and particular sensory changes during treatment [@seretny2014].

## Open questions

- No prevention has proven effective, and evidence for treating established painful CIPN is limited, with duloxetine the only agent recommended by the American Society of Clinical Oncology [@colvin2019].
- Standardised assessment is needed to establish how common painful (as opposed to painless) CIPN is and which patients are most vulnerable [@colvin2019; @seretny2014].
