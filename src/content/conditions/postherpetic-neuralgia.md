## Overview

After a chickenpox infection, the varicella-zoster virus stays dormant in sensory ganglia. When it reactivates it causes shingles: a painful rash in one dermatome, most often on the trunk or in the face. In some people the pain persists after the rash heals; this is postherpetic neuralgia [@johnson2014]. The risk rises with age, and more than 30% of people with postherpetic neuralgia still have pain after a year [@kawai2014].

## How it is classified

ICD-11 places it under **chronic peripheral neuropathic pain**, while the condition itself carries the zoster code 1E91.5 [@who2026; @scholz2019]. It is one of the clearest examples where the ICD-11 category and the IASP mechanistic descriptor coincide.

## Mechanisms

The virus damages sensory neurons and their axons. The pain, often burning or shooting, and touch-evoked pain (allodynia) in the affected skin reflect a combination of damaged and abnormally active peripheral nerve fibres and changes in central processing [@johnson2014].

## Open questions

- Why only a minority of people with shingles develop persistent pain, beyond age and the severity of the acute episode.
- How much of the ongoing pain depends on peripheral input versus central changes, which matters for choosing topical or systemic treatment.
