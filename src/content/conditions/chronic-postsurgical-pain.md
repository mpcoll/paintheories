## Overview

Persistent pain after surgery is common: 10–50% of people report it after operations such as groin hernia repair, breast and thoracic surgery, leg amputation and coronary artery bypass, and it is severe in about 2–10% [@kehlet2006]. Because it follows a controlled, planned injury, it is one of the few chronic pain conditions where the start can be studied prospectively and prevention can be attempted.

## How it is classified

ICD-11 introduced **chronic postsurgical pain** as a diagnosis in its own right, distinguishing tissue trauma from a controlled procedure (surgery) from accidental injury [@schug2019]. The pain must be localised to the surgical field, to the territory of a nerve in that area, or to a related dermatome.

## Mechanisms

Iatrogenic nerve injury is probably the most important cause [@kehlet2006]. The share of neuropathic pain differs greatly by operation, in line with how likely the operation is to damage nerves [@haroutiunian2013]. The intensity of acute postoperative pain correlates with the risk of persistent pain, and only some patients with intraoperative nerve damage develop it, pointing to individual susceptibility [@kehlet2006].

## Open questions

- Whether aggressive early treatment of postoperative pain prevents chronic pain remains to be shown [@kehlet2006].
- Assessment of the neuropathic component varies widely between studies, making comparisons difficult [@haroutiunian2013].
