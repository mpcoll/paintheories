## Overview

Central post-stroke pain (CPSP) is neuropathic pain that develops after a stroke, felt in the body region whose brain territory was damaged, together with sensory abnormalities there [@klit2009]. It may affect half of the body or a smaller region [@scholz2019]. In a population-based Danish study the minimum prevalence of definite or probable CPSP among stroke survivors was 7.3%; many affected people had pinprick hyperalgesia, cold allodynia or brush-evoked dysaesthesia [@klit2011].

## How it is classified

In ICD-11, CPSP is a form of chronic central neuropathic pain (MG30.50), coded together with the stroke that caused it [@scholz2019; @treede2019]. Separating it from other pains common after stroke, such as shoulder pain, painful spasticity, headache and other musculoskeletal pain, is a practical difficulty and one reason its true prevalence is uncertain [@klit2009].

## Mechanisms

The usual combination of sensory loss and hypersensitivity in the painful area suggests two steps: deafferentation by the stroke, followed by hyperexcitability of the remaining central neurons [@klit2009]. Treatments that target neuronal hyperexcitability are the most effective so far, which fits this account [@klit2009].

## Open questions

- Why only a minority of people with similar strokes develop CPSP, and which lesion sites or pathways are necessary, remains unclear.
- Better prospective studies with clear diagnostic criteria are needed to establish how common CPSP is and how it develops over time [@klit2009].
