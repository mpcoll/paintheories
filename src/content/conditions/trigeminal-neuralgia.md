## Overview

Trigeminal neuralgia causes severe, stimulus-evoked, short-lasting stabbing attacks of pain in the face [@bendtsen2020]. Attacks start and stop abruptly, follow one or more branches of the trigeminal nerve, and are often set off by innocuous stimuli such as touching the face [@cruccu2016]. It is rare: population-based studies put the prevalence between 0.03% and 0.3%, with about three women affected for every man and the maxillary and mandibular branches most often involved [@detoledo2016].

## How it is classified

The 2016 classification, also adopted by ICHD-3, separates classical trigeminal neuralgia (with morphological changes of the nerve root from vascular compression), secondary trigeminal neuralgia (caused by an identifiable neurological disease such as a tumour or multiple sclerosis) and idiopathic trigeminal neuralgia (no cause found) [@cruccu2016; @ihs2018]. It also grades how certain the neuropathic diagnosis is, from possible to definite. ICD-11 codes the disease as 8B82.0; as a chronic pain condition it falls under chronic neuropathic orofacial pain (MG30.62), and the IASP Task Force lists it among the commonest forms of chronic peripheral neuropathic pain [@who2026; @scholz2019; @benoliel2019].

## Mechanisms

The prevailing account is focal demyelination of trigeminal sensory fibres, usually where the central part of the nerve root is compressed by an overlying blood vessel and less often in the brainstem or from multiple sclerosis plaques [@love2001]. Demyelinated axons lying close together without intervening glia can fire spontaneously and pass excitation to their neighbours, which would explain brief, triggered paroxysms; decompressing the root relieves the pain quickly in most patients with vascular compression [@love2001]. A neurovascular contact on MRI is common, however, and should guide surgical decisions rather than confirm the diagnosis [@bendtsen2020].

## Open questions

- The causes and pathophysiology are still incompletely understood [@bendtsen2020]: neurovascular contact is also found in people without facial pain, and the mechanism in idiopathic cases is unknown.
- Some patients also have continuous pain between paroxysms [@who2026]; whether this reflects a different or additional mechanism is unresolved.
