## Overview

Burning mouth syndrome is a chronic burning or dysaesthetic pain inside the mouth with no visible cause on examination [@ihs2018]. A meta-analysis estimated a prevalence of about 1.7% in the general population, higher in women and in people over 50 [@wu2021]; it is most frequent in postmenopausal women [@jaaskelainen2017]. Treatment is difficult; the best evidence supports topical and systemic clonazepam [@jaaskelainen2017].

## How it is classified

The diagnosis requires a normal-looking oral mucosa and a normal clinical examination, including sensory testing, with no other diagnosis that better explains the pain [@ihs2018]. ICD-11 places it among chronic primary headache or orofacial pain: pain treated as a condition in its own right, with distress or interference with eating, speaking and other oral functions, rather than as a symptom of another disease [@who2026; @nicholas2019].

## Mechanisms

One influential account regards primary burning mouth syndrome as neuropathic pain that lacks obvious clinical signs. With specialised tests of small sensory and taste fibres and of the trigeminal system, most patients fall into a peripheral or a central type, which differ in mechanism, treatment response and psychiatric comorbidity; hormonal and genetic factors are proposed to contribute [@jaaskelainen2017]. A systematic review found evidence for neuropathic involvement at several levels of the nervous system and for a link with anxiety and depression, and argued that because these components cannot be separated, burning mouth syndrome may be considered nociplastic pain [@orliaguet2021].

## Open questions

- Whether burning mouth syndrome is best described as neuropathic, nociplastic or a mixture that differs between patients is unresolved [@jaaskelainen2017; @orliaguet2021].
- Prevalence estimates vary widely between regions and study designs, so its true frequency is uncertain [@wu2021].
