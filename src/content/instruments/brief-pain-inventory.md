## Overview

The BPI was developed by Cleeland and colleagues for use in cancer pain [@tan2004; @keller2004] and has been translated and used worldwide [@cleeland1994]. Its psychometric adequacy was first established in cancer pain and has since been shown in several chronic non-cancer pain conditions [@dworkin2008]. The developers at MD Anderson recommend the short form, which is also the basis of the translations, and offer versions for diabetic peripheral neuropathy and herpes zoster.

The interference items fall into three groups: physical functioning (general activity, walking ability, normal work), emotional functioning (mood, relations with other people, enjoyment of life) and a single sleep item [@dworkin2008].

## Strengths and limits

The BPI separates how much it hurts from how much pain gets in the way: intensity and interference load on distinct factors, and interference tracks disability more closely than intensity does [@tan2004; @keller2004]. IMMPACT recommends either the BPI interference items or the MPI Interference Scale as a core measure of physical functioning when no well-accepted disease-specific measure exists; it notes that the MPI scale does not assess sleep, whereas the BPI has a sleep item [@dworkin2005]. Internal consistency is adequate for comparing groups [@keller2004]; for judging change in individual patients, IMMPACT offers only a provisional, distribution-based benchmark of about 1 point [@dworkin2008].
