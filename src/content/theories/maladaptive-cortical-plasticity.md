## Overview

The maladaptive plasticity model holds that chronic pain, phantom limb pain above all, is generated or maintained by reorganisation of cortical body maps. After arm amputation, the region of primary somatosensory cortex (S1) that represented the hand loses its input, and neighbouring representations such as the lip are thought to expand into it. Flor and colleagues reported that the extent of this shift correlated with phantom pain but not with non-painful phantom sensations [@flor1995]. They proposed that reorganisation is not merely a correlate of pain but contributes to it, interacting with factors such as pain before amputation and use of the residual limb or a prosthesis [@flor2006].

The model was extended beyond amputation to chronic back pain [@flor1997] and complex regional pain syndrome [@maihofner2003], and functional MRI showed that only amputees with phantom pain had a shift of the lip representation into the deafferented motor and somatosensory hand areas, scaling with pain [@lotze2001]. It provided a rationale for treatments that aim to "normalise" cortical representations, including sensory discrimination training, prosthesis use, mirror therapy and graded motor imagery [@flor2001; @moseley2012c].

## Historical context

The model built on animal work in the 1980s and 1990s showing that somatosensory maps reorganise after deafferentation, and on the observation that touching the face could evoke sensations referred to a phantom hand. Magnetoencephalography made it possible to measure map shifts non-invasively in patients. The model gave phantom pain, long treated as puzzling or psychogenic, a concrete neural explanation.

## Critiques

- The core evidence is correlational, from small samples, and relies on coarse measures of reorganisation (distances between dipole locations). Other clinical factors, such as compensatory use of the intact hand, could drive both the map changes and the pain [@makin2015].
- High-field imaging shows that the missing hand's representation persists, with detailed finger maps decades after amputation. This questions the premise that the deprived territory is simply taken over [@kikkert2016; @makin2020].
- Makin and colleagues proposed that persistent pain may *maintain* the local representation, reversing the proposed causal direction [@makin2013].
- Generalisation to other chronic pains outpaced the evidence. In CRPS, the main S1 finding (a smaller hand representation) holds across studies, but those studies carry a high risk of bias [@dipietro2013].

## Legacy

The model was highly influential and shaped a generation of cortically targeted rehabilitation approaches. Its central claim, that phantom pain arises from remapping of neighbouring representations into the deprived cortex, is now actively disputed. Current work emphasises preserved but disconnected representations, interactions with body perception and behaviour, and multiple contributing mechanisms beyond S1 [@makin2020].

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish three claims: maps change; map changes correlate with pain; map changes cause pain. The evidence weakens at each step.
- Use the debate between the Flor and Makin groups as a case study in how measurement choices (dipole distance vs. representational analyses at 7T) shape conclusions.
- Note that treatments thought to "reverse reorganisation" may help through other mechanisms.
