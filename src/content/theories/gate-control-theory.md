## Overview

Melzack and Wall proposed that the spinal cord does not simply relay nociceptive signals but gates them. Interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa regulate how much afferent activity reaches transmission cells, depending on the relative activity of large and small fibres and on descending control from the brain [@melzack1965]. Pain therefore depends on modulation at the first synapse, not only on the stimulus.

## Historical context

The theory resolved the specificity–pattern debate by keeping specialised fibres and patterned input but placing a modulating mechanism between them and perception. It made psychological factors physiologically respectable and is widely credited with launching modern pain science and multidisciplinary pain management [@moayedi2013; @mendell2014].

## Critiques

- The original circuit diagram was too specific to survive: Wall himself restated the theory, keeping its principles while conceding that the details were wrong [@wall1978].
- Critics argued that the theory became difficult to falsify once its circuit was loosened into general principles of "modulation" [@nathan1976].
- The specific circuit is now largely of historical interest, but modern genetic work has identified real dorsal horn gating circuits that differ in detail from the 1965 proposal [@mendell2014].

## Legacy

Its central claim, that nociceptive transmission is modulated by other input and by the brain, is now consensus. The detailed circuit is of historical interest. Melzack later extended the theory to the brain as the neuromatrix.

## Teaching notes

- Separate the *principle* (modulation of transmission) from the *specific circuit* (largely wrong).
- Useful as a case study in how a theory can be productive while partly incorrect.
