## Overview

Henry Beecher, an anaesthesiologist serving with the US Army in Italy, interviewed severely wounded soldiers shortly after injury and was struck by how little pain many of them reported [@beecher1946]. Later he compared them with civilian men undergoing surgery, who asked for pain relief far more often despite having comparable or lesser wounds. He concluded that the significance of the wound, not its extent, largely determined how much the patient suffered [@beecher1956]. For the soldier, a wound could mean release from combat and survival; for the civilian, surgery meant disruption, risk and loss.

The general claim, that the meaning and context of a stimulus shape the pain it produces independently of tissue damage, has since been tested experimentally. Identical stimuli hurt more when presented as more damaging [@arntz2004; @moseley2007b]. Pain becomes more tolerable when framed as beneficial, through endogenous opioid and cannabinoid systems [@benedetti2013]. It can even be experienced as pleasant when it represents relief [@leknes2013]. Belief systems can also modulate pain [@wiech2008].

## Historical context

Beecher's observations were published before modern pain theory and were influential evidence against a strict specificity view in which pain is proportional to injury. Together with phantom limb pain and other clinical puzzles, they were later widely cited as evidence that pain is centrally modulated. Beecher's interest in the role of meaning also fed his work on placebo responses. Wall later argued more generally that pain is only weakly connected to injury and more strongly to the body's state and needs [@wall1979].

## Critiques

- Beecher's comparisons were observational and uncontrolled. Soldiers and civilians may have differed in wound type, time since injury, setting, prior analgesia and selection, and the main outcome was the request for analgesia rather than a measure of pain intensity.
- Delayed onset of pain after injury also occurs in civilians who stand to gain nothing from their injury, so positive significance cannot be the whole explanation for injury without pain [@melzack1982].
- "Meaning" is a broad construct. Experiments manipulate quite different things (perceived damage, benefit, relative relief, religious belief), and the account does not specify a single mechanism unifying them.
- Effects of meaning on pain ratings could partly reflect changes in reporting rather than in experience. Physiological, neuroimaging and pharmacological findings argue against a purely reporting-based explanation [@benedetti2013; @leknes2013].

## Legacy

The idea that meaning shapes pain is now a basic assumption of psychological, biopsychosocial, predictive and protective accounts of pain, and underpins pain-education interventions. Rather than a stand-alone theory, it is best seen as a well-supported principle that later models try to explain mechanistically, for example as changes in appraised threat, expectation or the value of outcomes. In one such study, false visual feedback about head rotation changed the point at which neck movement became painful [@harvie2015].

## Teaching notes

- Beecher's soldiers are often over-quoted: discuss what the data were (requests for analgesia, interviews) and what they can and cannot show.
- Contrast meaning effects with expectancy (placebo) effects. They overlap but are not identical: meaning concerns what the stimulus signifies, expectancy what the stimulus will be like.
- Ask whether "meaning" changes the sensory intensity of pain, its unpleasantness, or both (compare the intensity and unpleasantness effects in the cue experiments).
