## Overview

Specificity theory treats pain as a sense in its own right, like vision or hearing. It builds on Müller's doctrine of specific nerve energies (each nerve produces a single kind of sensation whatever stimulates it). Von Frey mapped punctate "spots" on the skin that gave rise to touch, warmth, cold or pain, found pain spots to be the most numerous, and proposed free nerve endings as pain receptors [@vonfrey1894; @norrsell1999].

## Historical context

Schiff's observation that spinal cord lesions could dissociate pain from touch supported separate pathways. Von Frey's mapping in the 1890s formalised the theory, which competed with intensive theory (pain as excessive stimulation of any sense) for the following decades [@dallenbach1939; @moayedi2013].

## Evidence for

- Specialized nociceptors, responding selectively to noxious stimuli, were identified in single-unit recordings in the 1960s [@perl2007].
- Distinct spinal pathways (e.g. the spinothalamic tract) carry nociceptive information, and their section (cordotomy) can relieve pain.

## Evidence against and critiques

- The correspondence between spots and receptor types proposed by von Frey did not hold up anatomically [@norrsell1999].
- The theory does not explain why the same stimulus can produce very different pain in different contexts, pain without injury, or pain that persists after pathways are cut.
- Molecular and genetic work suggests that sensory quality reflects interactions across populations of afferents, not purely isolated lines [@ma2010].

## Legacy

Specificity theory was right that dedicated nociceptors and pathways exist, but wrong that these alone determine pain. Modern accounts distinguish *nociception* (neural processing of noxious stimuli) from *pain* (an experience). The labeled-line versus population-coding debate continues at the molecular level [@ma2010].

## Teaching notes

- Separate the defensible part (specialized nociceptors exist) from the overreach (one-to-one mapping between nociceptor activity and pain).
- Good contrast case with pattern theory: both make claims about how *quality* is coded.
