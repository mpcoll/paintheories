## Overview

William K. Livingston, a surgeon who treated causalgia and phantom limb pain, proposed in *Pain Mechanisms* (1943) that these conditions are maintained by abnormal activity in the spinal cord rather than by a lesion of pain pathways alone [@livingston1943]. He suggested that intense or prolonged input from a damaged nerve or tissue excites the internuncial pool, the interneurons of the spinal grey matter, and that closed chains of these neurons can keep re-exciting themselves. The reverberating activity summates with incoming impulses, so that ordinary input is felt as pain. It also spreads to motor and sympathetic neurons, producing spasm, vasomotor and sweating changes in the limb, which in turn generate more abnormal input.

The result is a self-perpetuating vicious circle. Pain can then persist after the original injury has healed, spread beyond the territory of the damaged nerve, and be triggered by emotional and other non-noxious influences. The theory also had a therapeutic corollary: breaking the circle at any point, for example with procaine injections or sympathetic blocks, might stop the reverberation.

## Historical context

Livingston drew on Lorente de Nó's physiological description of reverberating chains of internuncial neurons and on clinical experience with causalgia and phantom pain, which defied explanation by simple transmission along dedicated pathways. His theory is usually grouped with the pattern theories as a "central summation" account [@moayedi2013]. Melzack and Wall drew on it, alongside Noordenbos's sensory interaction theory, when formulating gate control [@melzack1965].

## Critiques

- The proposed reverberating circuits were hypothetical. Livingston did not identify them anatomically or physiologically, and closed-loop reverberation in the dorsal horn as a pain generator has not been demonstrated.
- Clinical support rested largely on uncontrolled observations of relief after blocks. Later controlled trials of sympathetic blockade have been small and inconclusive [@oconnell2016].
- The sympathetic arm of the vicious circle is not required in all pathological pain. Where abnormal central processing has been shown in patients, it was often maintained by ongoing peripheral input rather than by self-sustaining activity [@gracely1992].

## Legacy

Livingston's central summation idea anticipated modern concepts of activity-dependent central plasticity, including wind-up and central sensitization [@mendell1966; @woolf1983], and the notion that pain can outlast its peripheral cause. His emphasis on sympathetic involvement fed into the later concept of sympathetically maintained pain. The specific reverberating-circuit mechanism is of historical interest only.

## Teaching notes

- Compare the "vicious circle" with modern central sensitization: what has been confirmed (activity-dependent amplification) and what has not (self-sustaining reverberation)?
- Useful for discussing how clinical puzzles such as causalgia and phantom pain pushed theory beyond simple specificity.
