## Overview

This account summarises how a group of clinician-researchers, especially Moseley and Butler, have taught modern pain science to clinicians and patients. Pain is described as a conscious output of the brain. It is produced when the brain, drawing on all available information, judges that body tissue is in danger and that protective action is needed [@moseley2007]. Nociception is one input to that judgement, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient. Beliefs, context, previous experience and cues from vision or the social environment can also make the brain judge that danger is present.

Two clinical consequences follow. First, pain is a poor guide to the state of the tissues, especially when it persists, because the protective system can become sensitized and protect more than it needs to. Second, education that changes how a patient understands pain ("Explain Pain", or pain neuroscience education) should lower the threat value of pain and so reduce pain itself. Moseley and Butler describe the aim of this education as shifting a person's idea of pain from a marker of tissue damage to a marker of the perceived need to protect body tissue, using conceptual-change methods from educational psychology [@butler2003; @moseley2015b].

## Historical context

The account developed in physiotherapy in the late 1990s and early 2000s as a clinical translation of Melzack's neuromatrix theory [@moseley2003] and of work on central sensitization. It was popularised by the book *Explain Pain* [@butler2003]. Its authors present it as a practical application of the biopsychosocial model, distinct from cognitive-behavioural therapy and from the education given in early multidisciplinary programmes [@moseley2015b].

## Critiques

- "Protection" can be used to explain almost any change in pain after the fact, so the framework is hard to falsify unless the threat value of a cue is specified independently of the pain it produces.
- The slogan "pain is in the brain" can be heard by patients and clinicians as saying the pain is imaginary or that the tissues do not matter. Its authors stress that it does not deny a role for peripheral nociception [@moseley2015b].
- The idea that pain falls because beliefs change has mostly been tested with correlations and short-term outcomes. Trials of education alone show small effects on pain, so it is unclear how much of the benefit depends on reconceptualization rather than on the active treatment it is combined with.

## Legacy

The account is widely used in physiotherapy and pain education. Pain neuroscience education is often delivered with exercise and other active treatment rather than on its own. The trial evidence is mixed: effects on fear and catastrophizing are more consistent than effects on pain and disability. Experimental work on how context and visual cues change pain has fed into Bayesian and predictive-processing accounts of pain.

## Teaching notes

- Separate the scientific claim (pain depends on the brain's evaluation of threat, not just nociception) from the clinical claim (explaining this reduces pain). The first is widely accepted; the second has only modest support.
- Discuss why an account can be popular clinically while effect sizes in trials stay small.
- Compare with the neuromatrix and predictive-processing entries: what does "the brain evaluates danger" add to them, and what does it leave unspecified?
