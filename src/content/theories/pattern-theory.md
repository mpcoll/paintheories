## Overview

Nafe's "quantitative theory of feeling" proposed that all cutaneous qualities arise from the pattern and amount of neural activity rather than from distinct receptor types [@nafe1929]. Sinclair and Weddell argued that sensory endings are not specialised by modality, citing tissues (such as the cornea) in which a single morphological ending type gives rise to more than one sensation [@sinclair1955; @weddell1955]. Livingston added a central component: reverberating circuits in the spinal cord could sustain abnormal activity and explain persistent pain.

## Historical context

Pattern theory was a reaction against von Frey's specificity theory, whose proposed matching of skin spots to receptor types had proved hard to confirm histologically [@norrsell1999]. Clinical phenomena such as phantom limb pain and causalgia, which Livingston drew on, suggested central mechanisms beyond simple transmission along dedicated lines.

## Critiques

- Pattern theory did not specify the code (which spatial or temporal features of the input signal pain) clearly enough to be tested directly.
- Its negative claim (no receptor specialisation) was stated in absolute terms, so the discovery of nociceptors was taken as a refutation of the whole theory, although its positive claim, that sensory quality depends on patterns of activity across fibres, was not tested directly at the time. Population-coding accounts have since revived that positive claim [@ma2010].

## Legacy

Pattern theory's emphasis on patterns of input and central processing was combined with specificity theory's receptors in gate control theory [@moayedi2013].

## Teaching notes

- Present together with specificity theory as a thesis–antithesis pair resolved (partly) by gate control.
