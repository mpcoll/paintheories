## Overview

Sharp and Harvey reviewed the psychological theories of chronic pain and of PTSD. They concluded that the two conditions, which often follow the same traumatic event, are mutually maintaining [@sharp2001]. Once both are present, features of each keep the other going and make it worse.

They described several pathways for this. Attention is biased towards threat, including bodily sensations and trauma cues. Anxiety sensitivity amplifies fear of arousal and pain. Pain acts as a reminder of the trauma. Both conditions rely on avoidance as a coping style. Depression and reduced activity are common to both. Managing two conditions also makes heavy cognitive demands, leaving fewer resources for adaptive coping. Asmundson and colleagues presented mutual maintenance alongside the shared vulnerability model as the two main explanations of the comorbidity [@asmundson2002].

## Historical context

The model came from cognitive-behavioural research on trauma, at a time when PTSD and chronic pain were studied by separate research and clinical communities. It drew on cognitive models of PTSD and on fear-avoidance and cognitive-behavioural models of chronic pain, and argued that the overlap was clinically under-recognised [@sharp2001]. Integrated treatments that combine cognitive processing therapy for PTSD with cognitive-behavioural therapy for pain were later developed for people with both conditions [@otis2009].

## Critiques

- The model lists many pathways but does not say which matter most or how they combine, so almost any cross-lagged pattern can be read as partial support.
- Most tests use cross-lagged panel models with few waves. A systematic review noted that methods vary widely and are not always suited to testing mutual maintenance [@ravn2018]. Classic cross-lagged models also cannot separate stable between-person differences from within-person reciprocal effects, and the results depend on the timing of assessments.
- Mutual maintenance and shared vulnerability are seldom tested against each other, although they make different predictions about what happens before the trauma.

## Legacy

The model is the most-cited framework for PTSD–pain comorbidity and is regularly tested in injury, burn, whiplash and military cohorts. The evidence supports an entangled relationship in which hyperarousal and intrusion symptoms seem especially important, but it does not consistently show the symmetrical reciprocity the model predicts [@ravn2018].

## Teaching notes

- Use a cross-lagged panel diagram to show what "mutual maintenance" predicts, and what an asymmetric result (PTSD → pain only) would mean.
- Discuss why a treatment study in which PTSD change precedes pain change is informative but still not proof of a causal pathway.
- Contrast with the shared vulnerability model: which findings would favour one over the other?
