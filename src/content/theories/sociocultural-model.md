## Overview

Sociocultural accounts hold that pain cannot be fully understood apart from the social and cultural world in which it occurs. Cultural groups teach different meanings for pain and different ways of showing and responding to it. These learned patterns affect what people report, how they behave, when they seek help and how others respond [@zborowski1952]. Bates's biocultural model states a strong version: culture does not only change how pain is described but, through learned attitudes, beliefs and emotional states, changes the pain intensity people perceive and report [@bates1987; @bates1993].

Recent work treats "culture" less as fixed ethnic traits and more as social experience and structural position. Group differences in pain are linked to discrimination, trust, socioeconomic factors, coping styles and unequal access to treatment [@campbell2012; @anderson2009]. Clinicians' beliefs are part of the system: false beliefs about biological differences between racial groups are associated with underestimating pain [@hoffman2016]. Historical and anthropological work adds that the meanings given to pain change across cultures and historical periods [@morris1991], and that illness narratives show how chronic pain is lived within families and institutions [@kleinman1988].

## Historical context

Zborowski's study of veterans' hospital patients in New York is the classic early empirical study of culture and pain [@zborowski1952]. Medical anthropology, especially Kleinman's distinction between disease and illness and his work on illness narratives [@kleinman1988], gave the field its concepts. Morris's cultural history of pain argued against treating pain as a purely medical problem [@morris1991]. From the 2000s, research on racial and ethnic disparities in pain care moved the focus to structural factors and clinician bias [@anderson2009].

## Critiques

- Early studies used small samples and described ethnic groups in terms close to stereotypes. Treating ethnicity as a proxy for culture runs together culture, socioeconomic status, discrimination and ancestry.
- Group differences in laboratory pain sensitivity are robust, but meta-analyses cannot show which social, psychological or biological factors cause them [@rahimwilliams2012; @kim2016].
- Sociocultural accounts are frameworks rather than mechanistic theories. They say that culture matters but often not by what route it changes pain.

## Legacy

These accounts underpin research on pain disparities, culturally informed pain assessment and the "social" part of the biopsychosocial model. Neuroimaging studies now test sociocultural hypotheses directly by asking which brain processes mediate group differences in pain [@losin2020].

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish differences in how pain is expressed, in how it is perceived, and in how others assess and treat it. Each needs different evidence.
- Use the laboratory findings and the clinician-bias findings together to discuss why "group differences in pain" can never be read simply as biology.
- Discuss the risks of describing cultural groups by fixed traits.
