## Overview

Developed from Melzack's work on phantom limbs and set out in a 1990 paper [@melzack1990], neuromatrix theory proposes that the brain contains a network that generates the experience of a unified body-self. Pain is one of its outputs, produced by a characteristic pattern of activity (the neurosignature), and may be triggered by sensory input, by cognitive and emotional processes, or by intrinsic activity [@melzack1999; @melzack2001].

## Critiques

- The theory is broad and difficult to falsify: it does not specify the neurosignature, the network's boundaries or how either could be measured.
- The theoretical neuromatrix is often identified with the empirical "pain matrix" of neuroimaging. Because that network also responds to salient non-painful stimuli, critics argue that its activity cannot be read as a pain-specific signature, which leaves the neuromatrix without an agreed empirical marker [@iannetti2010].
- Phantom phenomena are compatible with a central generator but do not establish one, because they can also be driven by ongoing input from injured peripheral nerves [@vaso2014].

## Legacy

The model has been influential in clinical education and rehabilitation [@moseley2003]. The neuromatrix moved the explanatory focus from the spinal cord to the brain and from input to output, a shift taken up by later network, predictive and "pain as protective output" accounts.

## Teaching notes

- Contrast "pain as input" (specificity) with "pain as output" (neuromatrix).
- Discuss the distinction between the theoretical neuromatrix and the empirical "pain matrix".
