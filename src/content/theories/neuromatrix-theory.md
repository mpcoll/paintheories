## Overview

Developed from Melzack's work on phantom limbs, neuromatrix theory proposes that the brain contains a network that generates the experience of a unified body-self. Pain is one of its outputs, produced by a characteristic pattern of activity (the neurosignature), and may be triggered by sensory input, by cognitive and emotional processes, or by intrinsic activity [@melzack1999; @melzack2001].

## Evidence for

- Phantom limb pain and pain after complete spinal cord injury show that pain can occur without corresponding peripheral input.
- Neuroimaging shows that pain is associated with activity in a distributed set of regions rather than a single centre.
- The model has been influential in clinical education and rehabilitation [@moseley2003].

## Evidence against and critiques

- The theory is broad and difficult to falsify: it does not specify the neurosignature or how to measure it.
- The "pain matrix" of neuroimaging, often identified with the neuromatrix, responds strongly to salient non-painful stimuli, so it may reflect salience detection rather than pain-specific processing [@iannetti2010].

## Legacy

The neuromatrix moved the explanatory focus from the spinal cord to the brain and from input to output, a shift taken up by later network, predictive and "pain as protective output" accounts.

## Teaching notes

- Contrast "pain as input" (specificity) with "pain as output" (neuromatrix).
- Discuss the distinction between the theoretical neuromatrix and the empirical "pain matrix".
