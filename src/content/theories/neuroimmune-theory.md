## Overview

The neuroimmune theory holds that glial cells and immune cells are active participants in pathological pain rather than passive support. Watkins, Milligan and Maier proposed that glial activation is "a driving force" for pathological pain: after nerve injury, inflammation or infection, spinal microglia and astrocytes release pro-inflammatory cytokines and other mediators that amplify nociceptive transmission [@watkins2001; @watkins2003]. The framework was later broadened to a "neuroimmune interface" in which peripheral immune cells, glia and neurons signal to each other at every level of the neuraxis [@grace2014].

Ji, Berta and Nedergaard proposed that chronic pain can be viewed in part as a "gliopathy": maladaptive glial states maintain central sensitization through mediators such as BDNF, IL-1β, TNF and chemokines, and through loss of glial homeostatic functions [@ji2013]. Glia are proposed to contribute especially to the maintenance of pain and to its spread, and neuroinflammation is now also invoked for widespread pain conditions [@ji2018].

## Historical context

The theory emerged in the late 1990s and 2000s, when pain research focused almost entirely on neurons. Animal work showing that glial inhibitors and cytokine antagonists reversed pain behaviours, and the identification of specific microglia-to-neuron signalling pathways (P2X4 receptors, BDNF, disinhibition of lamina I neurons), made glia a credible new target for analgesic development [@tsuda2003; @coull2005].

## Critiques

- **Translation**: glial modulators that were effective in rodents have so far failed or shown only small effects in patients. Species differences in microglial biology are one proposed explanation [@landry2012].
- **Sex differences**: the microglial pathway that dominated the animal literature appears to be male-specific in mice, which questions generalisations drawn largely from male rodents [@sorge2015].
- **Human markers**: the main in vivo human evidence uses TSPO PET. In human microglia, TSPO expression does not increase with pro-inflammatory activation as it does in rodents, so higher TSPO signal may reflect glial density rather than an activated phenotype [@owen2017].
- "Activation" covers many glial states, some protective; the theory sometimes treats it as a single pro-nociceptive switch.

## Legacy

Neuroimmune contributions to persistent pain are well established in animal models and form a major research area, including sex-specific mechanisms and immune resolution pathways. Whether targeting glia helps patients remains unproven.

## Teaching notes

- Separate robust preclinical mechanisms from the still-unconfirmed therapeutic claim.
- Use the TSPO debate to discuss what a human "biomarker of glial activation" actually measures.
- Compare with nociplastic pain: neuroinflammation is one of several candidate mechanisms for pain without clear tissue damage.
