## Overview

Diathesis-stress models from psychopathology hold that disorder emerges when a pre-existing vulnerability (diathesis) meets a sufficient stressor. Banks and Kerns applied this logic to the high rates of depression in chronic pain [@banks1996]. They argued that chronic pain is a distinctive stressor, involving persistent pain, functional limitation, and medical, interpersonal and economic difficulties. Depression develops mainly when this stressor activates semi-dormant psychological vulnerabilities, such as depressogenic cognitive schemas, that were present before pain began.

Turk extended the framework to chronic pain and disability after traumatic injury such as motor vehicle accidents and work injuries [@turk2002]. In his model, predisposing factors such as anxiety sensitivity and a tendency to anticipate and avoid harm interact with the injury. This sets off a cascade of catastrophizing, causal attributions, low self-efficacy, fear-driven avoidance and operant reinforcement that maintains disability. Turk offered the model to explain why people with similar, often minor, injuries have very different outcomes.

## Historical context

By the early 1990s, studies had shown that depression and other psychiatric disorders are much more common in chronic pain populations than in the general population [@polatin1993]. There was disagreement about whether psychological disturbance precedes pain (the antecedent view) or results from it (the consequence view) [@fishbain1997]. The diathesis-stress framework offered a middle position: vulnerability is present before pain, but disorder appears only once pain adds sufficient stress. Prospective "high-risk" designs, such as following people from acute herpes zoster, were developed to study psychosocial antecedents before pain became chronic [@dworkin1992].

## Critiques

- The framework is broad. Almost any pre-existing characteristic can be called a diathesis and almost any pain-related burden a stressor, so it is hard to falsify as a whole.
- Its defining prediction, a statistical interaction between diathesis and stress, is rarely tested directly. Most studies test main effects of psychological predictors, which many other models also predict.
- Diatheses are often measured after pain onset, when they may already be changed by pain, and longitudinal studies show bidirectional pain–depression effects that a one-way diathesis-to-disorder sequence does not capture [@kroenke2011].

## Legacy

The diathesis-stress framing is standard in textbook accounts of why psychological factors predict the transition from acute to chronic pain, and Turk's version has been used to organise fear-avoidance and anxiety-sensitivity constructs into a sequence [@martin2010]. It is less a single testable model than a general framework that later risk-factor and vulnerability models work within.

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish the antecedent, consequence and diathesis-stress views of depression in chronic pain, and ask what kind of data each needs.
- Point out that "psychological factors predict chronic pain" is a main effect; the diathesis-stress model specifically predicts an interaction.
- Compare with the shared vulnerability model, which names one specific diathesis (anxiety sensitivity) for two outcomes.
