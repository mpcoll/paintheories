## Overview

Loeser proposed that what is loosely called "pain" consists of four components, usually drawn as concentric layers of an onion [@loeser1982]. At the core is *nociception*, the detection of tissue damage by transducers in skin and deeper tissues and its transmission by A-delta and C fibres. *Pain* is the perception that may follow. *Suffering* is a negative affective response that pain can produce but that can also arise from fear, anxiety, isolation or loss. The outer layer is *pain behaviour*: grimacing, limping, taking medication, seeking care or staying in bed. These are the only components an observer can see, and they are shaped by their consequences and by the social environment in which the person lives [@loeser1999; @loeser2000].

The layers are causally linked from the inside out but can come apart. Nociception can occur without pain, pain can occur without nociception, suffering can occur without pain, and pain behaviour can persist after the inner layers have changed. In chronic pain, what clinicians mostly see is suffering and pain behaviour, and the model implies that neither can be taken as a direct indicator of tissue damage.

## Historical context

The model came out of the multidisciplinary pain clinic at the University of Washington in Seattle, where Bonica, Fordyce and Loeser worked. It combined the physiology of nociception with Fordyce's operant concept of pain behaviour and with a clinical interest in suffering, which Cassel had separately argued is experienced by persons rather than bodies [@cassel1982]. Waddell's "Glasgow illness model" for low back pain likewise distinguished physical problem, pain, distress and illness behaviour [@waddell1987].

## Critiques

- The model is a descriptive taxonomy rather than a mechanistic theory. It says which components to distinguish, not how one layer produces the next or why they come apart in some people and not others.
- The concentric drawing suggests a one-way, inside-out causal order. Contemporary accounts stress top-down influences (expectation, context, learning) on how nociception is processed, which the picture does not show.
- "Suffering" and "pain behaviour" are broad categories with no agreed operational definition within the model, which makes it hard to test as a whole.

## Legacy

The distinction between nociception and pain is now standard and is reflected in the notes to the 2020 IASP definition of pain [@raja2020]. The onion diagram remains common in teaching and in interdisciplinary pain management as a way to explain why a treatment aimed at one layer may leave the others unchanged.

## Teaching notes

- Use the model to show the difference between nociception and pain, then ask what evidence would show that a given pain behaviour no longer depends on the inner layers.
- Compare with the biopsychosocial model: the onion model is more specific about pain but still does not specify mechanisms.
- The inside-out arrows are a simplification; set them against top-down accounts such as the neuromatrix and predictive processing.
