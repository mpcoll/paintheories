## Overview

Intensive theory holds that pain is a product of stimulus intensity and central summation rather than a dedicated sense. Erb proposed that any sensory stimulus could produce pain if strong enough. Naunyn reported that patients with tabes dorsalis felt pain after many rapidly repeated sub-threshold tactile stimuli, and Goldscheider proposed summation of input in the dorsal grey matter of the spinal cord [@goldscheider1894; @moayedi2013].

## Evidence for

- Temporal summation of repeated stimuli is a robust phenomenon, now studied as "wind-up" and temporal summation of pain.
- Clinical cases in which innocuous touch becomes painful (allodynia) show that intensity alone does not fix sensory quality.

## Evidence against and critiques

- Specialized nociceptors exist, contrary to the claim that pain needs no dedicated receptors.
- Many intense non-noxious stimuli (bright light, loud sound) are not painful in the somatosensory sense.

## Legacy

The idea of central summation survived in pattern theory, gate control and the concept of central sensitization.

## Teaching notes

- Present as one side of the specificity-versus-intensity debate that ran from the 1890s to the 1960s [@dallenbach1939].
