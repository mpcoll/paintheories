## Overview

Intensive theory holds that pain is a product of stimulus intensity and central summation rather than a dedicated sense. Erb proposed that any sensory stimulus could produce pain if strong enough. Naunyn reported that patients with tabes dorsalis felt pain after many rapidly repeated sub-threshold tactile stimuli, and Goldscheider proposed summation of input in the dorsal grey matter of the spinal cord [@goldscheider1894; @moayedi2013].

## Critiques

- The theory cannot explain why many intense non-noxious stimuli (bright light, loud sound) are not painful, or why pain has qualities distinct from intense touch or warmth.
- The debate with specificity theory was framed as a choice between dedicated receptors and central summation. Later work showed that both contribute, so the theory's lasting error was its denial of pain-specific receptors rather than its emphasis on summation.

## Legacy

The idea of central summation survived in pattern theory, gate control and the concept of central sensitization. Temporal summation of pain ("wind-up" in animals, temporal summation of second pain in humans) is now a standard measure in quantitative sensory testing, and clinical allodynia shows that innocuous input can become painful, although through central sensitization rather than intensity alone.

## Teaching notes

- Present as one side of the specificity-versus-intensity debate that ran from the 1890s to the 1960s [@dallenbach1939].
