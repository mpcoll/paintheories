## Overview

Eccleston and Crombez argued that the primary function of pain is to interrupt: pain captures attention, displaces current concerns and urges escape [@eccleston1999]. Attention to pain is therefore not a deficit but the normal result of a system designed to prioritise threats to the body. The question is not whether pain interrupts, but when and how strongly.

The model specifies two groups of moderators. Pain-related characteristics (intensity, novelty, unpredictability and threat value) increase interruption. Characteristics of the person and environment, especially catastrophic thinking about pain, emotional arousal and the demands of competing tasks, determine whether pain or the current task controls attention [@eccleston1999]. Catastrophizing was later linked specifically to difficulty disengaging attention from pain [@vandamme2004].

In chronic pain, the same mechanism operates against a background of persistent signals. Hypervigilance, defined as a habitual readiness to detect pain or bodily threat, is proposed to maintain repeated interruption and to contribute to the cognitive complaints and disability that accompany chronic pain [@crombez2005].

## Historical context

The model built on Eccleston's application of cognitive attention theories to chronic pain [@eccleston1994] and on experimental primary-task paradigms developed by the authors in the 1990s, in which participants respond to tones while receiving painful stimuli [@crombez1996]. It reacted against treating attention to pain as a symptom to be corrected, and against capacity-only accounts that ignored the threat value of pain.

## Critiques

- "Interruption" and "threat value" are broad constructs, and interference effects depend strongly on the task used, which makes the model hard to falsify with any single paradigm [@moore2012].
- The model says more about what moderates capture than about how attention is subsequently controlled; later neurocognitive and motivational accounts added explicit top-down control by goals [@legrain2009; @vandamme2010].
- Tests of hypervigilance in chronic pain rely largely on attentional bias tasks whose effects are small and depend on procedural choices such as stimulus type and exposure time, so null results are hard to interpret as evidence against the model [@crombez2013].

## Legacy

The model is the standard reference for the claim that pain demands attention and has shaped experimental work on attentional interference, hypervigilance and attentional bias in pain. It led directly to the neurocognitive model of attention to pain and to motivational accounts in which pain competes with other goals [@legrain2009; @vandamme2010]. Cognitive effects of pain in patients and animals are reviewed in [@moriarty2011].

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish *interruption* (a momentary capture of attention by pain) from *attentional bias* (a stable preference for pain-related information); the evidence is stronger for the former.
- Discuss why the same capture that is adaptive for acute injury may become costly when pain persists.
- Compare with distraction research: if pain demands attention, when should distraction work?
