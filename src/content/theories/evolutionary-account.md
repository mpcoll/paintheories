## Overview

Evolutionary accounts ask why organisms have the capacity for pain at all, and why it is shaped as it is. Their starting point is that nociception and pain protect the organism. They motivate escape from tissue damage, avoidance of situations that caused it, and behaviour (rest, guarding) that helps recovery. People who cannot feel pain show what happens without it [@nesse2019].

Several further claims are made. Injury produces long-lasting sensitization of defensive responses in many species, from molluscs to mammals, which suggests strong and ancient selection pressures [@walters2019; @crook2014]. Nesse and Schulkin argue that much apparently excessive pain is normal: if pain is cheap and missed tissue damage is costly, selection should favour a system that often signals danger when there is none (the smoke-detector principle). They also argue that mechanisms that lower pain thresholds after repeated injury may make the system vulnerable to chronic pain [@nesse2019]. Williams questions the view that chronic pain is simply the inevitable cost of an alarm system. She suggests it may partly reflect a mismatch: when others meet an injured person's needs and an injured part is protected from normal movement, the recovery phase and sensitization may be prolonged [@williams2016]. She has also proposed that the facial expression of pain is an evolved signal that recruits help from others [@williams2002].

## Historical context

Sherrington introduced the concept of the nociceptor and described pain as the "psychical adjunct of an imperative protective reflex" [@sherrington1906]. Wall later argued that pain is better described as awareness of a need state, tied to recovery, than as a simple signal of injury [@wall1979]. Modern evolutionary work combines comparative studies of nociception across invertebrates and vertebrates with the clinical framework of evolutionary medicine. A 2019 Royal Society meeting brought these lines together [@walters2019].

## Critiques

- Adaptive explanations are easy to construct after the fact. A trait can be explained as an adaptation, a by-product or a mismatch with the modern environment, and it is often hard to test which explanation applies to chronic pain.
- Comparative work mostly measures nociceptive behaviour and sensitization; whether these involve anything like conscious pain in other species is still debated, and the definition of pain across species is unresolved [@walters2019].
- Evolutionary accounts explain why the capacity for pain exists but say little directly about mechanisms or about why a particular person develops chronic pain.

## Legacy

Evolutionary reasoning underlies the widespread view that acute pain is protective, which is central to the interruptive-function, fear-avoidance and protective-output accounts. Experimental tests of the adaptive value of sensitization and comparative studies of nociceptive mechanisms are an active research area, and evolutionary medicine offers a vocabulary (trade-offs, false alarms, mismatch) for discussing chronic pain.

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish questions about function and evolutionary history from questions about mechanism; the two are complementary.
- Congenital insensitivity to pain is a powerful example, but it shows the value of nociception, not necessarily of conscious pain.
- Ask students whether chronic pain should be called "maladaptive", and what evidence would decide.
