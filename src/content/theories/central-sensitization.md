## Overview

Woolf showed in rats that hypersensitivity after peripheral injury depended on changes in the spinal cord: increased excitability of the flexion reflex persisted even when input from the injured area was blocked [@woolf1983]. Central sensitization is defined as an increased responsiveness of nociceptive neurons in the central nervous system to their normal or sub-threshold input. It depends on activity-dependent synaptic plasticity, including NMDA-receptor-mediated mechanisms, and is amplified by neuroimmune signalling [@woolf2000; @latremoliere2009; @ji2018].

## Evidence for

- Extensive animal work characterises the cellular and molecular mechanisms [@latremoliere2009].
- In humans, secondary hyperalgesia, allodynia and enhanced temporal summation are observed in experimental models and in many chronic pain conditions [@woolf2011].

## Evidence against and critiques

- In humans, central sensitization is inferred indirectly (e.g. from quantitative sensory testing), and these measures are not specific to spinal mechanisms; brain-level processes such as attention and expectation can produce similar findings.
- The term is used loosely in clinical practice, sometimes as an explanation for any unexplained pain. This partly motivated the introduction of "nociplastic pain" as a descriptor that makes no specific mechanistic claim [@kosek2016].

## Legacy

Central sensitization is a central concept in the shift from seeing chronic pain as a symptom of tissue damage to seeing it as a state of the nervous system.

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish the *mechanism* (well established in animals) from the *clinical construct* (applied to patients by inference).
- Compare with peripheral sensitization, which involves increased responsiveness of nociceptors themselves.
