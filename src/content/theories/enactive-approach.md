## Overview

Enactive approaches apply ideas from enactive cognitive science to pain. Stilwell and Harman argue that pain is not something the brain produces and then sends to consciousness. It is enacted by a living, embodied organism as it makes sense of its situation, and that situation includes the physical environment, other people and cultural practices [@stilwell2019]. On this view, the meaning of pain to the person is not added to a sensory core; it is part of what the pain is.

Coninx and Stilwell developed the approach for the distinction between acute and chronic pain. Using the idea of a *field of affordances*, the possibilities for action that a person perceives in a situation, they describe acute pain and the transition to chronic pain as changes in that field. Behavioural learning, neural reorganisation and sociocultural practices are treated as interacting contributors to the change [@coninx2021]. Clinically, this suggests treatment should aim to reopen possibilities for meaningful action rather than target separate biological, psychological and social "factors".

## Historical context

The approach builds on enactivism in philosophy of mind and phenomenology (Varela, Thompson and others) and on work on embodied and embedded cognition. In pain research it was proposed explicitly as a way to go beyond the biopsychosocial model. Its authors argue that this model, as used in practice, divides people into separate biological, psychological and social parts and so keeps a mind–body dualism [@stilwell2019]. Related proposals combine enactive ideas with predictive processing [@kiverstein2022].

## Critiques

- The approach is so far a philosophical and clinical framework. It has made few specific, testable predictions, and much of the evidence cited for it fits equally well with other context-sensitive accounts such as predictive processing or the protective-output account.
- Concepts such as sense-making and affordance are hard to operationalise and measure in pain research.
- Critics of the critique may reply that the biopsychosocial model was always meant to describe interacting systems, so the difference between the two may lie more in emphasis than in substance.

## Legacy

This is a recent and growing strand of the philosophy of pain, mainly discussed in physiotherapy, rehabilitation and philosophy of cognitive science. Its main influence so far is on how clinicians think about patients' lived experience and on calls for less fragmented models of chronic pain.

## Teaching notes

- Contrast "the brain produces pain" (neuromatrix, protective output) with "the organism enacts pain in its environment". Ask what observation could tell the two apart.
- The field-of-affordances idea gives a concrete way to discuss disability: what can this person no longer see as possible?
