## Overview

The cognitive-behavioural model treats people with chronic pain as active processors of information, not passive recipients of nociceptive input or environmental contingencies. What people believe about pain shapes how they feel and act, and their actions in turn affect their bodies and the responses of others. Relevant beliefs include what pain means, whether it signals harm, whether it can be controlled and whether they can cope [@turk1983; @turk1992]. The model is transactional. Cognition, emotion, behaviour, physiology and the social environment influence one another over time, so that unhelpful appraisals and coping can maintain pain-related disability and distress.

Cognitive-behavioural therapy follows from this. It helps patients reconceptualise pain as a problem they can manage, teaches and rehearses coping skills, and works to generalise and maintain gains [@turk1983]. Self-efficacy, perceived control and catastrophizing are the cognitive variables most often proposed as the routes by which treatment works [@turk1992; @turner2007]. Turk and Rudy also used psychological assessment data to derive subgroups of patients (dysfunctional, interpersonally distressed and adaptive copers) [@turk1988]. This taxonomy was later used to argue for matching treatment to patient profiles.

## Historical context

The model emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s as cognitive approaches entered behaviour therapy. It drew on Meichenbaum's work on self-instruction and stress inoculation [@turk1983]. It responded to the limits of the purely operant model, which its proponents argued neglected the cognitive and psychophysiological sides of pain [@turk1987]. Gate control theory had already made it plausible that cognition could modulate pain.

## Critiques

- CBT for pain is a heterogeneous package. Which components and processes produce change is unclear, and effect sizes have not grown across decades of trials [@morley2013; @williams2020].
- The model includes many reciprocally interacting variables. This makes it flexible but hard to falsify, and it specifies few precise mechanisms.
- Putative cognitive mediators change in treatments that do not target them, which challenges the specificity of the model's account of how CBT works [@smeets2006; @turner2016].
- Proponents of acceptance-based approaches argue that trying to change the content of thoughts, or to control pain, may be less useful than changing how people relate to them [@mccracken2014b].

## Legacy

The cognitive-behavioural model is the dominant framework for psychological pain treatment, and CBT has the largest trial evidence base of any psychological therapy for chronic pain [@williams2020; @ehde2014]. Later psychological models, including the fear-avoidance and psychological flexibility models, developed from or in dialogue with it.

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish the model (how appraisals and behaviour maintain disability) from the treatment package (CBT); evidence on one does not automatically transfer to the other.
- Small average effects can hide variation between patients. Discuss what "very small effect versus active control" means when the control is itself a credible treatment.
- Contrast with the operant model: same behaviours, different explanation of why they persist.
