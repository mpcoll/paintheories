## Overview

Imperativism holds that pain tells us to do something rather than telling us how things are. Klein proposed that the content of pain is a command to protect a part of one's body, such as "don't put weight on that ankle!" [@klein2007]. Hall proposed a parallel account of itch, whose content is the command to scratch [@hall2008]. Martínez argued that the painfulness of pain can itself be explained by its imperative content [@martinez2010]. Martínez and Klein used signalling theory to argue that pain signals are mainly imperative [@martinez2015].

In his book, Klein places pain among homeostatic sensations, which have the function of motivating the organism to restore or protect its condition. Grahek treated pain asymbolia as a counterexample to motivational accounts of pain [@grahek2007]. Klein replies that asymbolics' indifference shows nothing about pain's intrinsic nature. In his view it shows a breakdown in how pains relate to the subject, which he ties to a loss of concern for one's own bodily integrity [@klein2015; @klein2015b].

## Historical context

Imperativism arose in the 2000s within representationalist philosophy of mind, in response to the difficulty perceptual theories had with pain's motivational force. It connects with neuroscientific work describing pain as a homeostatic emotion and with functional and evolutionary accounts of pain as a protective drive.

## Critiques

- Evaluativists argue that imperative content does not explain why pain feels bad: a command can be received without its hurting, and pain seems to give reasons for action rather than only demand it [@bain2012].
- Corns argues that unpleasantness and motivational force doubly dissociate, which undermines accounts that explain painfulness through motivation [@corns2014].
- Some pains (for example pain with no possible protective response, or chronic pain long after healing) do not clearly command anything that would help, so imperativists need an account of such cases.

## Legacy

Imperativism is a main position in current philosophy of pain, with evaluativism as its chief rival. It fits well with functional accounts in pain science that emphasise motivation and action, such as homeostatic, interruptive and motivation–decision accounts.

## Teaching notes

- Ask what a pain "says": a description ("damage here") or a command ("protect this").
- Use pain asymbolia to compare imperativist and evaluativist explanations of what asymbolics lack.
