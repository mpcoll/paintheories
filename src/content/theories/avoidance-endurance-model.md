## Overview

The avoidance-endurance model (AEM) adds a second maladaptive pathway to the fear-avoidance account of chronic pain. Some people respond to pain with catastrophizing, fear and avoidance, which leads to physical underuse. Others *endure* pain. They keep going with tasks despite severe or rising pain, suppress or minimise pain-related thoughts, and stop only when pain becomes intolerable. Endurance responding is proposed to cause physical overload and to maintain or increase pain [@hasenbring2000; @hasenbring2010].

The model distinguishes subgroups. Fear-avoidance responders show catastrophizing and avoidance. Distress-endurance responders combine thought suppression, anxious or depressed mood and persistence. Eustress-endurance responders combine ignoring or minimising pain with positive mood and persistence. Adaptive responders show none of these patterns [@hasenbring2010; @hasenbring2012]. Responses are measured with the Avoidance-Endurance Questionnaire [@hasenbring2009]. The clinical implication is that people at risk need different treatments: more activity for avoiders and better regulation of activity for endurers.

## Historical context

The model came from prospective studies of patients with lumbar disc problems, in which endurance strategies were among the psychological predictors of persistent pain [@hasenbring1994]. It was formulated around 2000 and developed in the following decade [@hasenbring2000; @hasenbring2010]. It reacted against the dominance of the fear-avoidance model, arguing that reduced activity cannot explain disability in all patients and that overactivity also needs to be explained [@hasenbring2019].

## Critiques

- "Endurance" combines adaptive task persistence with harmful overactivity, and findings depend on which is measured [@andrews2012; @kindermans2011].
- Subgroups are formed from a small number of self-report scales, such as thought suppression, behavioural endurance and depression [@hasenbring2012]. Their stability over time and replicability across samples need further testing.
- The proposed overload mechanism, in which endurance leads to repetitive strain of muscles, ligaments and vertebral segments, is supported mainly by indirect neuromuscular and activity data [@hasenbring2019].
- Most evidence comes from back pain samples and from one research group. Independent prospective tests are fewer and smaller in effect [@morf2026].

## Legacy

The AEM made overactivity and endurance a recognised target in pain assessment and rehabilitation, alongside avoidance [@hasenbring2019]. It has encouraged risk-stratified, subgroup-specific psychological treatment, and endurance patterns are now studied beyond back pain, for example in acute neck pain [@morf2026].

## Teaching notes

- Contrast the AEM with the fear-avoidance model: both predict disability, but through opposite activity patterns (underuse vs overuse).
- Distinguish task-contingent persistence (often adaptive) from pain-ignoring overactivity (proposed to be harmful). This distinction explains many mixed findings.
- Discussion prompt: why might activity pacing, often recommended for endurers, be associated with more pain in cross-sectional studies?
