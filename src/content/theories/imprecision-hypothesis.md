## Overview

Moseley and Vlaeyen proposed that chronic pain can be understood, in part, as a learned protective response that has become imprecise [@moseley2015]. Pain is always accompanied by other information: the movement being made, the posture of the body, touch, what is seen, the context and the person's thoughts. Through associative learning, these cues can come to predict and then evoke pain. The hypothesis applies to pain itself the learning principles that the fear-avoidance tradition applied to pain-related fear.

The key word is *imprecision*. Learning is adaptive when the protective response is tied precisely to cues that signal danger. When encoding is imprecise, the response generalises to cues that merely resemble the original ones, and the set of triggers grows: more movements, positions and contexts evoke pain, without any change in nociceptive input [@moseley2015]. Experimental work on fear generalisation in pain provides the main analogue for how such overgeneralisation could occur [@meulders2013; @meulders2015].

The hypothesis implies a treatment target. If pain is maintained by imprecise associations, then interventions should restore precision, for example by graded exposure that teaches the difference between cues that predict danger and cues that do not.

## Historical context

The proposal came from the convergence of two research lines: Moseley's work on how body representation and non-nociceptive cues modulate pain, and Vlaeyen and Meulders's work on pain-related fear acquisition and generalisation in movement-based conditioning paradigms [@meulders2011; @meulders2013]. It reacted against accounts that explain chronic pain solely through nociceptive or central sensitisation mechanisms, and it parallels perceptual accounts linking poor discrimination to overgeneralised fear [@zaman2015].

## Critiques

- Evidence that pain itself, rather than pain expectancy or fear, can be classically conditioned in humans remains limited; conditioned hyperalgesia effects are small and conditioned allodynia is unproven [@madden2015].
- Many patient studies use hypothetical scenarios or measure pain expectancy and fear, not pain, so they test overgeneralised beliefs more directly than overgeneralised pain [@meulders2014; @harvie2020].
- Cross-sectional differences between patients and controls cannot show whether imprecise learning precedes chronic pain or results from it [@harvie2017].
- "Imprecision" is defined qualitatively; formal statistical accounts of pain inference offer one way to make it measurable [@tabor2017].

## Legacy

The hypothesis is widely cited as a framework for learning-based explanations of chronic pain and for exposure and discrimination-based treatments. It stimulated research on conditioned modulation of pain by tactile, proprioceptive and visual cues [@harvie2016; @madden2016b] and on generalisation of pain-related fear and avoidance in patient groups.

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish generalisation of *fear* and *expectancy* (well supported) from generalisation of *pain* (less directly tested).
- Use the bogus visual feedback study as an example of non-nociceptive cues shifting pain [@harvie2015].
- Ask students what longitudinal study would test whether imprecise learning predicts the transition to chronic pain.
