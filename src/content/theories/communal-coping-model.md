## Overview

Catastrophizing, an exaggerated negative orientation towards actual or anticipated pain, is one of the strongest psychological correlates of pain and disability. Sullivan and colleagues reviewed theoretical accounts of why: schema activation, appraisal, attention and communal coping [@sullivan2001]. The communal coping account treats catastrophizing as a way of coping that serves *social* goals. By expressing distress and pain more intensely, the person in pain seeks closeness, help and empathy from others, rather than trying to reduce the pain on their own.

Thorn, Ward, Sullivan and Boothby set out the communal coping model in more detail [@thorn2003]. On this view, catastrophizing can make pain worse in two ways. Others' responses may maintain pain expression, and the person may use fewer self-directed coping strategies. Sullivan and colleagues proposed levels of explanation: social goals may explain why catastrophizing develops and persists, while appraisal processes may explain how it affects pain experience [@sullivan2001].

## Historical context

By the late 1990s, catastrophizing had been firmly linked to pain outcomes, but research had gone ahead without much theory [@sullivan2001]. Most explanations were intrapersonal, concerning threat appraisal and attention to pain. The communal coping model gave catastrophizing an interpersonal function, drawing on the broader idea of communal coping (coping as a joint activity within close relationships) from the stress and coping literature. It led to a series of studies in the 2000s that manipulated observer presence or measured partner responses.

## Critiques

- The model's predictions overlap with operant accounts. Both expect social responses to maintain pain behaviour, so findings on solicitous responses rarely discriminate between them.
- Catastrophizing questionnaires do not measure the social goals the model attributes to catastrophizers, so the proposed motive is inferred, not measured.
- Most clinical tests are cross-sectional and rely on patients' perceptions of others' responses rather than observed behaviour.
- Experiments in children and adults suggest that pain expression in high catastrophizers depends little on who is watching, and that threat appraisal and social context shape expression in ways the model does not predict [@vervoort2008; @vlaeyen2009].

## Legacy

The communal coping model drew attention to the interpersonal side of catastrophizing and contributed to dyadic and couple-based pain research, including diary studies of patients and spouses [@burns2015]. Evidence for its specific claims is mixed. Catastrophizing is reliably associated with pain expression and with how others respond, but not consistently with solicitous responses, and it is often related to negative ones [@boothby2004; @buenaver2007]. Sullivan and colleagues had themselves suggested that appraisal and communal accounts work at different levels of explanation and may be complementary rather than competing [@sullivan2001].

## Teaching notes

- Contrast the appraisal and communal coping accounts and design an experiment (e.g. observer present vs absent × high vs low threat) that could separate them.
- Discuss why a correlation between catastrophizing and punishing partner responses is hard to interpret causally.
