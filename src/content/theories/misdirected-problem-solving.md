## Overview

Eccleston and Crombez proposed that worry about chronic pain is best understood not as a symptom of anxiety but as problem solving [@eccleston2007]. Pain interrupts and is appraised as a problem. Worry is the repeated search for possible solutions. In acute pain this is adaptive: the problem is soluble and worry motivates seeking help or avoiding harm.

The problem-solving goes wrong when the problem is framed in a way that cannot be solved. If pain is construed as a sign of damage whose only solution is relief or cure, and relief does not come, each failed solution renews worry, increases vigilance to pain and bodily sensations and prompts further searching [@aldrich2000; @eccleston2007]. Worry is "misdirected" because it is aimed at an unachievable goal, not because the person's reasoning is defective.

The model's therapeutic implication is that the problem itself needs reframing, from "how do I get rid of pain?" to "how do I live a valued life with pain?". This links it to dual-process models of coping, in which assimilative strategies (changing the situation) are contrasted with accommodative ones (adjusting goals) [@vandamme2008].

## Historical context

The model developed from the authors' work on attention and the interruptive function of pain, and from a review applying research on generalised worry to chronic pain [@eccleston1999; @aldrich2000]. A diary study then described the content and characteristics of pain-related worry in patients [@eccleston2001]. The authors developed a questionnaire of assimilative and accommodative solutions to pain and a goal-based, motivational view of coping [@devlieger2006; @vandamme2008].

## Critiques

- The model was published as a short topical review. Direct experimental tests of the full worry–failed solution–worry loop are few, and most evidence is cross-sectional or comes from healthy volunteers.
- The distinction between worry, catastrophizing and rumination is not sharply drawn; related accounts recast catastrophizing itself as a form of repetitive negative thinking with an emotion-regulating function [@flink2013].
- Whether a problem is "soluble" is often only known in hindsight, so classifying a given solution-seeking effort as misdirected risks circularity.

## Legacy

The model is cited mainly as a theoretical bridge between attentional and fear-avoidance accounts and acceptance-based approaches. It helped motivate experimental work on the costs of trying to control pain and on goal pursuit and goal conflict in pain [@crombez2008b; @notebaert2011; @vandamme2008].

## Teaching notes

- Contrast "worry as pathology" with "worry as problem solving aimed at the wrong goal": the second implies reframing goals rather than suppressing worry.
- Link to acceptance and commitment therapy: why might reducing efforts to control pain improve functioning?
- Ask students to design a prospective test that distinguishes misdirected problem solving from a general disposition to worry.
