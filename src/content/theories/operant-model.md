## Overview

Fordyce applied operant (Skinnerian) learning principles to chronic pain. He separated nociception and the private experience of pain from *pain behaviours*: the observable ways people communicate pain, such as complaining, grimacing, guarding, resting and taking medication. Pain behaviours may begin as responses to nociception, but they can come under the control of their consequences. They are positively reinforced by attention and solicitous care, negatively reinforced by escape from aversive or demanding activity, and may be maintained while well behaviours go unreinforced [@fordyce1973; @fordyce1976]. On this view, disability can outlast tissue healing because the contingencies, not the injury, now sustain the behaviour.

The model's treatment follows directly from this analysis. Staff and family withdraw attention from pain behaviour and reinforce activity. Exercise is set by quotas that rise gradually rather than by pain. Medication is given on a fixed schedule rather than on demand [@fordyce1973; @gatzounis2012]. The aim is to change behaviour and function, not to abolish pain.

## Historical context

The model grew out of behaviour therapy in the late 1960s and early 1970s, at a time when the dominant medical view treated pain reports as signs of tissue damage to be found and removed [@fordyce1976]. It offered an explanation for disability that was out of proportion to findings, and a treatment that did not depend on finding a lesion. Early solicitous-spouse studies [@block1980; @flor1987] and an application to acute back pain [@fordyce1986] extended it beyond the inpatient programme. The model was soon criticised for neglecting cognition and was absorbed into cognitive-behavioural approaches [@turk1987].

## Critiques

- The pain behaviour construct leaves out cognitive, psychophysiological and medical factors. Treating pain behaviour as the problem risks an incomplete understanding of the patient and inappropriate treatment [@turk1987].
- Most early support came from treatment outcomes. These show that pain behaviour changes, not that reinforcement established or maintained it [@jolliffe2004].
- Experimental tests mostly reinforce verbal pain reports over a single session, often in healthy volunteers. How far this models the slow development of pain behaviour in daily life is an open methodological question.
- The solicitous-spouse hypothesis reduces a complex relationship to reinforcement. Later work places partner responses within marital satisfaction, intimacy and communication [@leonard2006; @newtonjohn2006].
- Language about "pain behaviour" and withdrawing attention has been criticised for implying that patients' pain is not genuine. Fordyce's model concerns behaviour, not whether pain is real.

## Legacy

Its treatment techniques, notably graded activity, activity pacing and time-contingent medication, remain part of interdisciplinary pain management and cognitive-behavioural programmes [@gatzounis2012]. Operant learning is one of the two learning mechanisms (with Pavlovian learning) in current behavioural accounts of chronic pain, which emphasise that pain motivates action to control harm [@vlaeyen2020]. Experimental work has extended the model from pain behaviour to perception: in healthy volunteers, reinforcing short-term sensitization can shift pain sensitivity without awareness of the contingencies [@holzl2005].

## Teaching notes

- Separate the three things Fordyce separated: nociception, pain experience and pain behaviour. Ask which one each study in the evidence list actually measures.
- A common misreading is that the model says pain is "learned" or not real. It makes claims about the maintenance of behaviour, not about whether pain is genuine.
- Discussion prompt: why might solicitous partner responses predict worse function in some couples and not in others?
