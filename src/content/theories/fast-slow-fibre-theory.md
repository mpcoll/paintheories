## Overview

In his 1959 monograph, the Dutch neurosurgeon Willem Noordenbos argued that pain cannot be understood as a dedicated line from receptor to brain [@noordenbos1959]. Cutaneous input arrives through a fast system of large myelinated fibres and a slow system of small fibres, and what is felt depends on how the two interact centrally. Fast-fibre activity normally restrains the transmission of slow-fibre activity, an idea often summarised as "fast blocks slow". Slow-fibre input was thought to travel through a diffuse, multisynaptic chain of spinal neurons in which it could summate.

The theory was built to explain clinical pain, especially postherpetic neuralgia, in which light touch can provoke delayed, burning, poorly localised pain that outlasts the stimulus. Noordenbos attributed these features to a selective loss of large fibres, which removed fast-system inhibition and let slow-system activity build up.

## Historical context

Noordenbos wrote during the debate between specificity and pattern theories. Like the pattern theorists, he stressed patterns and central summation of input. Unlike them, he accepted that fibres of different sizes have different roles. His "sensory interaction" theory was one of the main sources of Melzack and Wall's gate control theory, which kept the fast–slow interaction and placed it in a specific dorsal horn circuit [@melzack1965; @moayedi2013].

## Critiques

- The theory specified an interaction but no mechanism for it. Gate control filled that gap, and its own circuit was later revised [@mendell2014].
- The idea that pathological pain follows from a shifted ratio of large to small fibres was not borne out in later clinical and pathological studies of neuropathy [@dyck1976; @nathan1976].
- The slow, multisynaptic spinal pathway was described in general terms and could not be tested directly with the methods of the time.

## Legacy

The principle that non-nociceptive input can inhibit nociceptive transmission survives in modern accounts of segmental inhibition and in treatments such as TENS and spinal cord stimulation. The specific "fibre ratio" explanation of neuropathic pain has been abandoned. Noordenbos is mainly remembered as a direct precursor of gate control theory.

## Teaching notes

- A useful bridge between pattern theory and gate control: it keeps fibre classes, but makes their interaction, not their identity, decisive.
- Ask students why the "fibre ratio" prediction failed while the inhibition principle survived.
