## Overview

Gifford proposed the mature organism model as a conceptual model for clinicians, especially physiotherapists, who treat people in pain [@gifford1998]. It is set out as an input–processing–output scheme. The organism continuously samples its tissues and its environment. The central nervous system evaluates this input against what the person has learned, believes and expects. It then produces a set of outputs: pain, changes in movement and motor control, sympathetic and neuroendocrine responses, and behaviour. The outputs are coordinated to protect the tissues and promote recovery.

The word *mature* emphasises that the evaluation is done by an organism with a long history. Previous injuries, cultural beliefs and the current situation all shape how the same tissue state is interpreted. The model therefore asks clinicians to consider both what is happening in the tissues and how the person's nervous system is responding to it.

## Historical context

The model was developed in British physiotherapy in the 1990s, when clinical reasoning was moving away from purely tissue-based explanations of musculoskeletal pain. It brought contemporary pain neuroscience, including the idea that pain is one of several outputs of a central evaluating system, into physiotherapy clinical reasoning. Its framing was later taken up in pain-education approaches.

## Critiques

- The model is a heuristic for clinical reasoning rather than a theory that makes specific predictions. Few studies have tested it directly, and the evidence listed here is consistent with it rather than designed to test it.
- Treating every output as part of one protective response may overstate how coordinated those responses are. Motor responses to experimental pain, for example, vary considerably between muscles and people [@hodges2003].
- Its main ideas were later absorbed into broader frameworks (the neuromatrix, the protective-output account), so it is now mainly of historical and educational interest.

## Legacy

The model's input–processing–output framing and its emphasis on the protective function of pain survive in physiotherapy education and in the "pain as a protective output" account. Gifford's own writing is mainly cited in physiotherapy rather than in experimental pain research.

## Teaching notes

- Use it to show physiotherapy students that pain and motor changes can be read as outputs of the same evaluating system.
- Ask what result would count against the model. Its looseness is both its strength in the clinic and its weakness as science.
