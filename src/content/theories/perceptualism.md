## Overview

Perceptual theories treat feeling pain as perceiving something about one's own body. Armstrong analysed bodily sensations as perceptions of bodily states [@armstrong1962]. Pitcher argued that to feel a pain in one's foot is to perceive a part of one's body as being damaged or disturbed [@pitcher1970]. Representationalists such as Tye developed this view: the phenomenal character of a pain is fixed by its representational content, typically tissue damage of a certain kind, intensity and location [@tye1995].

The view explains why pains have locations and intensities, and why they can be non-veridical (phantom-limb pain, referred pain) in the way visual experiences can be illusory. Its main difficulty is *painfulness*. A purely sensory representation of tissue damage does not obviously explain why pain is unpleasant and motivating. Later versions add evaluative content (that the damage is bad for the subject) [@cutter2011], which brings them close to evaluativism.

## Historical context

Mid-20th-century philosophy of mind tried to replace "private object" views of sensation with accounts that place pains in the public, physical world. Perceptual theories were one answer. Representationalism became prominent in the 1990s as a general theory of phenomenal consciousness, and pain was seen as a hard test case.

## Critiques

- Pain does not behave like ordinary perception: people do not usually take a pain to be a perception of something that could exist unperceived, and "pain" names the experience rather than its object.
- Representationalism has particular difficulty with affect. It must explain why pain is unpleasant, why unpleasantness can change while sensory content stays the same, and how unpleasantness motivates [@aydede2013].
- Clinical dissociations such as pain asymbolia have been used both to support separating sensory from affective components of pain and to challenge accounts on which painfulness consists in sensory content [@grahek2007].

## Legacy

Perceptual and representationalist views remain a main position in the philosophy of pain and the starting point for evaluativist and imperativist theories, which keep the representational framework but change the content. For pain science they are relevant to how intensity, location and unpleasantness are measured and whether they should be treated as separate dimensions.

## Teaching notes

- Compare pain with vision: what would a "pain illusion" be, and are phantom-limb pain and referred pain examples?
- Use the hypnosis and lesion studies to ask whether sensory content alone could explain why pain hurts.
