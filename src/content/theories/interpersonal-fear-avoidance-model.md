## Overview

The interpersonal fear-avoidance model (IFAM) extends the fear-avoidance model of chronic pain from the individual to the parent–child dyad. It was outlined by Goubert and Simons in a chapter on cognitive styles and processes in paediatric pain [@goubert2013]. Simons and colleagues formally tested it when they developed a measure of parents' pain-related fear and avoidance [@simons2015].

Parents watching their child in pain go through their own fear-avoidance cycle. Catastrophic thoughts about the child's pain raise parental fear and distress. These motivate protective and avoidant behaviour, such as stopping activities, keeping the child home from school, or giving special attention to pain. These parental responses interact with the child's own catastrophizing, fear and avoidance, which together are proposed to maintain functional disability in the child.

## Historical context

By the early 2010s, research had established that the adult fear-avoidance model applies to children and adolescents with chronic pain, with some developmental differences [@simons2012]. Parental catastrophizing had been linked to parents' urge to stop their child's painful activity [@caes2011]. Asmundson and colleagues proposed a paediatric fear-avoidance model with parent factors at around the same time [@asmundson2012]. The IFAM made the parent's own fear-avoidance process explicit and linked it step by step to the child's.

## Critiques

- Most tests are cross-sectional or have short follow-ups and use path models in which the proposed ordering is assumed rather than tested. Effects of the child on the parent are rarely modelled.
- Parents' and children's reports share method variance, and pooled associations between parent factors and child outcomes are mostly small [@donnelly2020].
- Tests have mostly involved mothers of youth recruited from clinical services [@birnie2020; @neville2020]. How well the model applies to fathers, community samples and acute pain is less clear.
- The model's main clinical implication, that changing parental fear or protectiveness improves child outcomes, has rarely been tested directly as a mechanism of change.

## Legacy

The IFAM is a central framework in paediatric pain psychology and has guided parent-focused assessment, such as the Parent Fear of Pain Questionnaire [@simons2015]. Recent work extends it with intolerance of uncertainty and parental physical and mental health [@neville2020; @birnie2020]. Related proposals consider how a child's chronic pain may change the parent's brain and behaviour over time [@simons2016].

## Teaching notes

- Draw the adult fear-avoidance cycle and then add a parallel parent cycle. Ask where the two cycles connect.
- Discuss how to tell whether parental protectiveness causes child disability or responds to it.
