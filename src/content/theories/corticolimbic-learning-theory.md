## Overview

Apkarian, Baliki and Geha proposed that chronic pain is best understood as a persistent state of learning. Nociceptive input after injury interacts with corticolimbic circuits, and in vulnerable people this produces lasting reorganisation in which pain-related memories are continually reinforced instead of extinguished [@apkarian2009]. The key structures are the nucleus accumbens and the medial prefrontal cortex, which together compute the value and salience of events, along with the amygdala and hippocampus.

The theory makes two linked claims. The first is a *predisposition* claim: corticolimbic properties present early in the course of back pain, or before it, predict who will develop chronic pain [@baliki2012; @vachonpresseau2016]. The second is a *transformation* claim: as pain becomes chronic, it is represented less in nociceptive regions and more in emotional and limbic circuitry, so that chronic pain comes to resemble a negative emotional state [@hashmi2013]. Baliki and Apkarian later placed this within a broader account in which nociception, pain, negative mood and behaviour selection are distinct but interacting processes, with mesolimbic circuitry central to the transition [@baliki2015].

## Historical context

The theory grew out of brain-imaging work in chronic back pain in the 2000s, which showed grey-matter and functional differences in prefrontal and limbic regions, and out of the recognition that spinal sensitization alone did not explain chronic pain in humans. It drew on learning and addiction neuroscience, where the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex are central to reinforcement and habit.

## Critiques

- The key prospective imaging studies come largely from one research programme and used modest samples. Effect sizes in small brain-wide association studies tend to be inflated, so large independent replications are needed.
- Large population data suggest that brain and other biological markers alone predict self-reported chronic pain poorly, and that psychosocial factors carry much of the predictive information [@fillingim2025].
- "Learning" is used broadly. The theory does not always specify which learning process (Pavlovian, instrumental, extinction failure) is altered or how it would be measured behaviourally.
- Most human data come from chronic back pain, so generalisation to other conditions is uncertain.

## Legacy

The theory shifted attention from spinal and peripheral mechanisms to forebrain learning and motivation circuits as determinants of chronic pain. It is a major source of candidate brain biomarkers of chronification, and it links pain research to work on reward, depression and addiction [@borsook2016; @kuner2016].

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish the *predisposition* claim (who becomes chronic) from the *transformation* claim (how pain changes once chronic).
- Compare with the fear-avoidance and conditioning models, which describe learning at the behavioural level.
- Discussion prompt: if pre-existing brain properties predict chronic pain, what does that imply for prevention?
