## Overview

Evaluativism is a representationalist account of what makes pain unpleasant. Bain argues that a pain experience represents a bodily disturbance and also represents that disturbance as bad for the subject. This evaluative content is what makes the pain unpleasant [@bain2012]. Cutter and Tye defend a similar "tracking" representationalism, in which painfulness consists in representing tissue damage as bad [@cutter2011].

The view explains dissociations between sensory and affective aspects of pain. Patients with pain asymbolia, who report pain but appear not to mind it, are described as having experiences with the sensory content but not the evaluative content: pains that do not hurt [@bain2013]. Evaluativists also argue that evaluative content explains why unpleasant pains give reasons for action and motivate.

## Historical context

Evaluativism developed in the 2000s and 2010s as philosophers tried to fit the affective dimension of pain, emphasised by pain scientists since Melzack and Casey, into representationalist theories of experience. It was proposed as an alternative to imperativism and to accounts that treat unpleasantness as a non-representational feeling or as a desire. Bain reviews the family of evaluativist accounts and the objections to them [@bain2017].

## Critiques

- Critics argue that representationalist accounts, evaluativism included, struggle to explain how representing something as bad makes an experience feel bad and gives the subject a reason to act [@aydede2013].
- Corns argues that unpleasantness and motivational force come apart, and that even together they are not sufficient for painfulness. This challenges evaluativist and imperativist accounts that tie these features closely together [@corns2014].
- The interpretation of pain asymbolia is disputed. It is not clear whether asymbolics lack evaluative content, lack motivation, or lack a more general concern for their own bodies [@grahek2007].

## Legacy

Evaluativism is one of the main current positions on pain affect in philosophy. For pain science, it gives a precise statement of the hypothesis that unpleasantness is a separate component of pain that can vary independently of sensory intensity.

## Teaching notes

- Contrast "this is damage and it is bad for me" (evaluativism) with "stop this!" (imperativism) as accounts of what pain says.
- Ask how pain-unpleasantness ratings in experiments relate to the philosophers' notion of evaluative content.
