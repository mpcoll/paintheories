## Overview

The psychological flexibility model applies the model behind acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) to chronic pain [@hayes2006; @mccracken2014]. It proposes that much of the suffering and disability in chronic pain comes from psychological inflexibility rather than from pain itself. Inflexibility means efforts to avoid, control or eliminate pain and pain-related thoughts and feelings, and being dominated by these thoughts. These efforts often fail to reduce pain and crowd out valued activity. Psychological flexibility, the alternative, is built from six interrelated processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, values and committed action [@mccracken2014; @mccracken2014b].

Pain acceptance has been the most studied process. It is defined as engaging in activities regardless of pain and being willing to experience pain without trying to control it [@mccracken2004]. ACT aims to improve functioning by increasing flexibility. Reducing pain is not its main target, so the model predicts larger effects on functioning than on pain intensity.

## Historical context

ACT grew out of behaviour analysis and is linked to relational frame theory, a programme of basic research on language and cognition [@hayes2006]. McCracken and colleagues studied pain acceptance from the late 1990s [@mccracken1998; @mccracken2004]. McCracken and Morley then proposed psychological flexibility as a model to integrate and update psychological approaches to chronic pain, arguing that cognitive-behavioural approaches needed a more unified, process-oriented model to make further progress [@mccracken2014; @mccracken2014b]. It is often grouped with mindfulness-based approaches as a "third wave" of CBT.

## Critiques

- ACT has not been shown to outperform traditional CBT. Across conditions, its advantage over cognitive or behavioural treatments was small and non-significant [@ost2014], and acceptance- and mindfulness-based interventions for pain have not proved superior to CBT [@veehof2016].
- Flexibility processes overlap with each other and with constructs from other models such as catastrophizing and self-efficacy, so it is unclear whether they are distinct mechanisms [@turner2016].
- Many process measures resemble outcome measures, for example questionnaires about activity engagement despite pain, which risks circularity in mediation analyses.
- Much of the trial evidence is small and of low methodological quality [@hughes2017; @williams2020].

## Legacy

ACT is now one of the main psychological therapies evaluated for chronic pain, and acceptance is widely studied as a process of change in pain treatment generally [@hughes2017; @akerblom2015]. The model has shifted treatment goals from pain reduction towards valued functioning.

## Teaching notes

- Acceptance in this model is not resignation. It means stopping unworkable struggle with pain in order to act on what matters.
- Compare the targets of ACT (relationship to thoughts, values-based action) with CBT (content of appraisals, coping skills), then compare their evidence. Are the models more different than the outcomes?
- Discussion prompt: what evidence would show that flexibility is a distinct mechanism rather than another measure of good functioning?
