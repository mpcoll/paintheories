## Overview

Lethem and colleagues proposed that people with pain differ in how they respond to it, from confrontation to avoidance, and that avoidance explains why some people develop exaggerated pain and disability [@lethem1983]. Vlaeyen and Linton set out the cognitive-behavioural version that became standard: a cycle in which pain interpreted catastrophically leads to fear, avoidance and hypervigilance, then to disuse, disability and depression, and back to pain [@vlaeyen2000; @vlaeyen2012].

## Critiques

- Much of the supporting evidence is cross-sectional, which cannot establish the causal sequence (catastrophizing → fear → avoidance → disability) that the model proposes [@leeuw2006].
- The model treats avoidance largely in isolation; later work argues it should be placed in the context of people's competing goals and motivations [@crombez2012].
- Its linear, cyclical structure has been criticised as too simple to capture individual pathways.

## Legacy

The fear-avoidance model is one of the most influential psychological models of chronic pain and the basis of graded exposure. Recent work links it to learning theory, for example the proposal that imprecisely encoded threat associations generalise and maintain pain [@moseley2015].

## Teaching notes

- Draw the cycle and ask students where each intervention (education, exposure, activity) acts.
- Discuss the difference between cross-sectional associations and evidence of causal pathways.
