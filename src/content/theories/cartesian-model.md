## Overview

Descartes' *L'Homme* (written around 1633, published posthumously) contains the famous illustration of a kneeling boy whose foot is near a fire. Particles of fire displace the skin, which tugs a thread running inside the nerve to the brain; this opens a valve that lets animal spirits flow back to the muscles. The account treats pain as the end-point of a dedicated transmission line, a picture that shaped later physiology [@moayedi2013; @perl2007].

## Historical context

The model belongs to Descartes' mechanistic account of the body as a machine, with the mind interacting with it in the brain. It is less a theory of pain specifically than an illustration of reflexes and sensory transmission.

## Critiques

- The model provides no mechanism by which attention, emotion, meaning or context could change pain, so it cannot accommodate pain without injury or injury without pain.
- Historians note that Descartes' own view of pain was more nuanced than the "hard-wired pathway" caricature, since he also discussed pain as a perception of the mind. Criticisms of "the Cartesian model" therefore often target a simplified reconstruction rather than Descartes' text.

## Legacy

The Cartesian picture is the usual starting point for the history of pain theories and the foil against which gate control and later theories defined themselves. The term "Cartesian" is now often used, somewhat loosely, for any account that equates pain with tissue damage.

## Teaching notes

- Use the fire illustration to introduce the idea of pain as a *signal* versus pain as a *perception*.
- Common misconception: that modern specificity theory and the Cartesian model are the same claim. Specificity theory concerns dedicated receptors and pathways; it does not require a fixed relation between stimulus and experience.
