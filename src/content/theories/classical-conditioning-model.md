## Overview

The classical (respondent, Pavlovian) conditioning account holds that pain is a powerful unconditioned stimulus. Stimuli that reliably occur with it come to predict it and elicit conditioned responses. Early behavioural analyses of chronic pain considered respondent as well as operant processes [@linton1984]. On the respondent view, cues associated with pain elicit anxiety and muscular or autonomic responses that can themselves add to pain. Respondent treatment, which targets these physiological responses (for example through relaxation), became one of three classic behavioural approaches to chronic pain, alongside operant and cognitive treatment [@henschke2010].

The contemporary version treats Pavlovian learning as the way people learn to *predict* harmful events, and operant learning as the way they learn to *control* them [@vlaeyen2015; @vlaeyen2020]. Movements, touch, visceral sensations and contexts paired with pain acquire threat value. Fear then generalises to similar stimuli and is reduced by extinction. Chronic pain disability is linked to anomalies in these processes, particularly impaired safety learning and excessive generalisation of fear [@meulders2020; @meulders2019]. A more contested claim is that pain itself, not only fear of pain, can be a conditioned response, so that cues alone could amplify or elicit pain [@madden2015].

## Historical context

The account applies a century of fear-conditioning research to pain, using paradigms in which movements or touch serve as conditioned stimuli and painful stimuli as unconditioned stimuli [@meulders2020]. In clinical pain psychology, operant treatment was more prominent in the 1970s and 1980s. Respondent ideas returned through the fear-avoidance model and experimental work from the 2000s onwards on fear of movement-related pain [@meulders2011; @vlaeyen2015].

## Critiques

- Most evidence comes from short laboratory studies in healthy volunteers. Whether the same processes operate over months in clinical pain is largely inferred [@harvie2017].
- Patient–control differences in conditioning are cross-sectional and could be consequences rather than causes of chronic pain [@harvie2017].
- Conditioning effects on pain are hard to separate from verbal suggestion and expectancy. Reviews have had to exclude studies that did not distinguish them [@madden2015].
- Whether conditioning can *elicit* pain (allodynia), as opposed to amplifying it, is unresolved [@madden2015; @madden2016].
- Associative and propositional (expectancy-based) accounts of the same findings make overlapping predictions, so it is unclear which learning process does the work.

## Legacy

Associative learning is central to current behavioural accounts of chronic pain and to exposure-based treatment [@vlaeyen2020; @meulders2020]. It also underlies the imprecision hypothesis, which proposes that imprecisely encoded associations between cues and pain generalise and maintain pain [@moseley2015].

## Teaching notes

- Distinguish three claims: cues can elicit *fear* of pain (well supported), cues can *amplify* pain (moderately supported), and cues can *elicit* pain without nociception (unresolved).
- Contrast prediction (Pavlovian) with control (operant), and ask which a given treatment, such as exposure or graded activity, targets.
- Discussion prompt: what design would show that altered conditioning precedes, rather than follows, the onset of chronic pain?
