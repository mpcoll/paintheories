## Overview

Social pain overlap theory holds that being excluded, rejected or bereaved "hurts" in more than a metaphorical sense. It proposes that the distress these experiences cause depends partly on the neural systems that process the unpleasantness of physical pain, especially the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and anterior insula, and on shared neurochemical systems such as opioids [@eisenberger2003; @eisenberger2012]. MacDonald and Leary gave the evolutionary argument: for social mammals, separation from caregivers and the group threatened survival, so the system that protects against physical harm was co-opted to protect social bonds [@macdonald2005].

The theory predicts two-way links between the two kinds of pain: people more sensitive to one should be more sensitive to the other; social support should reduce physical pain; and analgesics should reduce social pain [@eisenberger2012]. Later work extended the overlap from affective to sensory regions when rejection is intense [@kross2011].

## Historical context

The theory brought together a long tradition of describing social loss in the language of pain, animal work on separation distress and opioids, and the new neuroimaging of pain affect that linked the anterior cingulate to unpleasantness. The 2003 Cyberball study made it a major topic in social neuroscience [@eisenberger2003].

## Critiques

- Overlapping activation in a region does not show a shared process, because the anterior cingulate and insula respond to many salient, aversive or attention-demanding events. Inferring "pain" from their activation is reverse inference [@iannetti2013]. Lieberman and Eisenberger used large-scale meta-analytic reverse inference to argue that the dorsal anterior cingulate is selective for pain [@lieberman2015]. Wager and colleagues disputed that interpretation [@wager2016].
- Iannetti and colleagues argue that the mechanisms of social and physical pain differ, and that the overlap lies in general-purpose systems for detecting salient and threatening events rather than in pain-specific ones [@iannetti2013].
- The Cyberball paradigm produces mild, brief exclusion, and behavioural findings such as analgesic effects on social pain rest on a small number of studies.

## Legacy

The theory is influential in social psychology and social neuroscience and has encouraged research on social factors in clinical pain, such as loneliness and social support. In pain neuroscience it is contested. Multivariate studies suggest that physical pain and social rejection share gross anatomy but not fine-grained neural representations [@woo2014].

## Teaching notes

- A good case for teaching the difference between overlapping activation and a shared representation, and the problem of reverse inference.
- Ask what kind of evidence would show that social pain is pain rather than a different aversive state that uses some of the same circuitry.
