## Overview

Spinal manipulative therapy covers high-velocity, low-amplitude thrust techniques (manipulation) and low-velocity, low-amplitude techniques (mobilisation) [@rubinstein2019]. The evidence below is for chronic low back pain.

## Evidence

A 2019 meta-analysis of 47 trials (9211 participants) with chronic low back pain found that manipulation gives short-term pain relief similar to other recommended therapies, and small, not clinically important benefits on pain compared with non-recommended therapies; results were similar at 6 and 12 months [@rubinstein2019]. It did improve back-specific function by a small, clinically relevant amount compared with recommended therapies (SMD -0.25, 95% CI -0.41 to -0.09). Evidence against sham manipulation was of low to very low quality, so effects beyond sham remain uncertain [@rubinstein2019].

## In practice

NICE allows manual therapy for low back pain only within a package that includes exercise [@nice2016]; the American College of Physicians lists it among first-choice non-drug treatments, on low-quality evidence [@qaseem2017]. People should be told about the risk of transient adverse events [@rubinstein2019].

## Open questions

- How much of the effect is specific to the technique rather than to non-specific effects such as placebo and expectation [@bialosky2009]; only 5 of 47 trials in the 2019 meta-analysis tried to blind participants with a sham treatment [@rubinstein2019].
