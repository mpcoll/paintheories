## Overview

For many years rest and inactivity were advised for chronic pain; exercise is now offered across many conditions, as aerobic, strength, flexibility and balance training, or as yoga, Pilates and tai chi [@geneen2017]. The main aims are less pain, better physical function and better quality of life.

## Evidence

An overview of 21 Cochrane reviews found favourable but inconsistent effects on pain and mostly small to moderate improvements in physical function, with low-quality evidence because most trials were small and short [@geneen2017]. Condition-specific reviews give clearer estimates: in chronic low back pain exercise reduced pain by a clinically important amount compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo [@hayden2021]; in knee osteoarthritis land-based exercise reduced pain immediately after treatment (high-quality evidence), and a smaller benefit was sustained two to six months later [@fransen2015]; and in fibromyalgia aerobic training may slightly reduce pain [@bidonde2017]. Against other conservative treatments the advantage for back pain is small, and no difference was found against manual therapy [@hayden2021].

## In practice

NICE recommends offering exercise both for chronic primary pain [@nice2021] and for osteoarthritis [@nice2022], and exercise is the only 'strong for' therapy in the EULAR fibromyalgia recommendations [@macfarlane2017]. Because pain can flare when people start, NICE advises explaining that joint pain may increase at first and that regular exercise is still beneficial [@nice2022].

## Open questions

- Why exercise-induced hypoalgesia is impaired in some people with chronic pain, and whether this predicts who benefits from training [@rice2019].
- The mechanisms of exercise-induced hypoalgesia are unknown; one study in healthy adults pointed to a non-opioid, possibly endocannabinoid, mechanism after isometric exercise [@koltyn2014].
- Which type, dose and delivery of exercise works best for whom; trials are mostly small with short follow-up [@geneen2017].
