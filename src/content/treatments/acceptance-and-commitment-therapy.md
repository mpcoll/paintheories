## Overview

ACT grew out of behaviour therapy and applies the psychological flexibility model to chronic pain [@hayes2006; @mccracken2014]. Its goal is better functioning and valued activity, not lower pain scores, which matters when reading trials that use pain intensity as the main outcome.

## Evidence

The 2020 Cochrane review analysed only five ACT trials (443 participants). It found no evidence of a difference from active controls and, in two trials, a large benefit over treatment as usual for pain, but rated all ACT evidence as very low quality, so the estimates could easily change [@williams2020]. Larger meta-analyses that pool waiting list, usual care and active comparators report small effects on pain intensity and larger effects on functioning, acceptance and depression; none of these used GRADE [@ma2023]. An earlier meta-analysis of 11 trials found benefits for functioning, anxiety and depression but no significant effect on pain intensity, and warned that methodological limitations may have inflated effects [@hughes2017].

NICE recommended ACT for chronic primary pain partly on economic grounds: most of its evidence showed better quality of life and sleep and less pain and distress, and one economic evaluation found ACT cost effective [@nice2021].

## Open questions

- Most comparisons are against waiting list or usual care; head-to-head trials against CBT are few, and the Cochrane review could not separate the two [@williams2020].
- Whether gains in psychological flexibility explain the clinical benefit, as the model predicts, needs testing in trials designed to measure mediators.
