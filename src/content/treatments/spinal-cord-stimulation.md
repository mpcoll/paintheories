## Overview

SCS is offered as a last-resort treatment for refractory pain, for example painful diabetic neuropathy, complex regional pain syndrome and failed back surgery syndrome [@joosten2020]. Conventional (tonic) stimulation produces tingling paraesthesias over the painful area; newer subthreshold paradigms such as high-frequency and burst stimulation do not, which for the first time made double-blind placebo-controlled trials possible [@joosten2020].

## Evidence

The evidence splits by trial design. In open-label trials that added SCS to medical management or physical therapy, many more people reported at least 50% pain relief, although the evidence is of low or very low certainty [@oconnell2021]. In the PROCESS trial in failed back surgery syndrome with predominant neuropathic leg pain, 48% with SCS and 9% with medical management alone reached this at 6 months [@kumar2007]. Against placebo stimulation the effects are small and of very low certainty across chronic pain [@oconnell2021], and in chronic low back pain moderate-certainty evidence from one cross-over trial suggests SCS probably does not improve pain, function or quality of life at 6 months [@traeger2023]. The Cochrane review of low back pain concluded that its data do not support SCS outside a clinical trial [@traeger2023].

## In practice

Both kinds of trial have weaknesses: the Cochrane review of low back pain regarded the lack of placebo control in the add-on trials as an important source of bias, while the placebo-controlled cross-over trials mostly measured outcomes within a month and did not account for period and carry-over effects [@traeger2023]. Device complications and repeat surgery are part of the decision: in PROCESS, 32% of people with SCS had device-related complications by 12 months [@kumar2007].
