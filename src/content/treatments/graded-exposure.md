## Overview

Graded exposure in vivo applies the fear-avoidance model to people with chronic pain who report substantial fear of movement or (re)injury. Avoidance protects in the short term but can be harmful in the long term, and exposure-based treatments aim to restore the pursuit of valued life goals despite persistent pain [@vlaeyen2020]. In early single-case experiments in chronic low back pain, pain-related fear and catastrophizing fell only during the exposure phase, not during graded activity, whichever came first [@vlaeyen2001].

## Evidence

Randomized trials have compared exposure with other active treatments, which makes effects harder to detect. In fearful patients with chronic low back pain, exposure reduced catastrophizing and perceived harmfulness of activities more than operant graded activity, but was not more effective for disability, pain intensity or activity levels up to 6 months [@leeuw2008]. Compared with CBT, exposure reduced movement-related disability on one scale but not pain intensity, and a short version worked faster than a long one [@glombiewski2018]. In complex regional pain syndrome type 1, exposure reduced disability more than pain-contingent usual care, and pain intensity also favoured exposure [@denhollander2016].

Meta-analyses are small. In chronic low back pain, graded exposure improved short-term disability and catastrophizing slightly more than graded activity [@lopezdeuraldevillanueva2016]. Across chronic musculoskeletal pain, eight trials showed improvements in pain-related fear, catastrophizing and some disability measures, with inconsistent effects on pain intensity and certainty of evidence from very low to moderate [@zhang2026].

## In practice

Exposure is aimed at people whose disability is driven by pain-related fear, and trials usually select them on high fear scores [@leeuw2008; @glombiewski2018]. Its main targets are fear and avoidance rather than pain intensity.
