## Overview

Pain science education, often called pain neuroscience education or Explain Pain, is a family of educational interventions grounded in educational psychology. Its developers present it as an application of the biopsychosocial model that is distinct from cognitive behavioural therapy and from the education given in early pain management programmes [@moseley2015b].

## Evidence

A mixed-methods review of 12 trials in chronic musculoskeletal pain found that effects on pain and disability had low clinical relevance, while the reduction in fear of movement in the short term was clinically relevant [@watson2019]. A meta-analysis restricted to chronic low back pain found moderate-quality evidence that adding education to physiotherapy improves disability in the short term, but no evidence of long-term benefit [@wood2018].

The RESOLVE trial tested 12 weekly sessions of education combined with graded sensorimotor retraining, designed to help people move and be active while experiencing back pain, against sham treatments and attention control in 276 people with chronic low back pain. Pain fell by 1.0 more point on a 0 to 10 scale at 18 weeks, an effect the authors call small [@bagg2022]. In a preplanned mediation analysis, the largest share of the effect on pain ran through changed beliefs about the consequences of back pain, followed by pain catastrophizing and pain self-efficacy [@cashin2023]. In acute low back pain at high risk of becoming chronic, two hours of intensive education about pain added to first-line care did not reduce pain at 3 months compared with placebo education [@traeger2019].

## In practice

NICE NG59 asks clinicians to give people with low back pain advice and information to help them self-manage, including information on the nature of low back pain and sciatica [@nice2016]; this is general advice, not a specific pain science education programme. Education is mostly used as one component of physiotherapy, exercise or psychological treatment rather than on its own.
