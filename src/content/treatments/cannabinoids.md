## Overview

Cannabinoids, cannabis and cannabis-based medicines are increasingly used for pain, with limited understanding of their efficacy and safety [@fisher2020]. Preclinical work on the endocannabinoid system is extensive and suggests real analgesic potential, but cannabinoids that work in animal models have been hard to translate into useful medicines [@finn2021].

## Evidence

The two largest recent syntheses agree that the effects in people are small. A BMJ meta-analysis of 32 trials found moderate to high certainty evidence that non-inhaled medical cannabis or cannabinoids give a small to very small improvement in pain, physical function and sleep compared with placebo, with no improvement in emotional, role or social functioning [@wang2021]. A review for the IASP task force, rating all outcomes as low or very low quality, concluded that the evidence neither supports nor refutes claims of efficacy and safety [@fisher2020]. Trials lasted at most 5.5 months [@wang2021], and important questions about long-term safety remain open [@haroutounian2021].

## In practice

In 2021 IASP stated that, because of a lack of evidence from high-quality research, it does not endorse the general use of cannabinoids for pain [@iasp2021]. NeuPSIG recommends against cannabinoids for neuropathic pain [@soliman2025]. A balanced reading is that some people may get a small benefit at the cost of common, mostly transient side effects, and that better trials are needed [@wang2021; @haroutounian2021].
