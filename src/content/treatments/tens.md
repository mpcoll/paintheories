## Overview

TENS is non-invasive and easy to apply, with relatively few contraindications [@sluka2003]. It is used for symptomatic relief in the moment, so the largest meta-analysis assessed pain during or immediately after a single session of strong but comfortable stimulation [@johnson2022].

## Evidence

The two main syntheses point in different directions. The meta-TENS review of 381 trials found pain intensity lower during or immediately after TENS than with placebo (moderate certainty), and lower than with standard-of-care treatments (low certainty), with no difference between acute and chronic pain [@johnson2022]. The Cochrane overview of chronic pain found the evidence within eight reviews consistently of very low quality, with small trials and important methodological limitations, and could not conclude whether TENS helps or harms [@gibson2019]. The two ask different questions: meta-TENS pooled acute and chronic pain and assessed the immediate effect [@johnson2022], whereas the Cochrane overview covered reviews in chronic pain conditions, which varied in whether they included studies of the immediate effects of single treatments [@gibson2019].

## In practice

NICE advises against TENS for chronic primary pain [@nice2021], low back pain [@nice2016] and osteoarthritis [@nice2022]. The Cochrane overview called for larger, better-designed trials to reduce the uncertainty about TENS in chronic pain [@gibson2019].
