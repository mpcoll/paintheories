## Overview

Paracetamol was for decades the recommended first analgesic for back pain and osteoarthritis because it is cheap and well tolerated at normal doses. Its mechanism remains uncertain: weak cyclooxygenase inhibition does not explain its effect well, and newer work points to a metabolite, AM404, acting on TRPV1 and cannabinoid receptors in the brain and spinal cord [@ohashi2020].

## Evidence

A 2015 meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials found high-quality evidence that paracetamol does not reduce pain or disability in the short term in people with low back pain (mostly acute), and gives a significant but not clinically important effect in hip or knee osteoarthritis [@machado2015]. The Cochrane review of osteoarthritis confirmed a reduction of about 3 points on a 0 to 100 scale, well below what patients notice [@leopoldino2019]. For chronic low back pain there is almost no trial evidence: the only trial was a single intravenous dose study that was later retracted [@saragiotto2016]. A network meta-analysis of NSAIDs and paracetamol in osteoarthritis concluded that there is no role for single-agent paracetamol, whatever the dose [@dacosta2017].

## In practice

NICE no longer recommends starting paracetamol for chronic primary pain [@nice2021], advises against paracetamol alone for low back pain [@nice2016] and against routine use in osteoarthritis [@nice2022]. Many people still take it, and the evidence suggests that its benefit in these conditions is at best small.
