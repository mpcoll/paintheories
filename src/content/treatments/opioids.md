## Overview

Opioid drugs act on the same receptors as the body's own opioids, partly through the brainstem system that normally controls pain transmission [@fields2004]. Their effect also depends on what people expect: in healthy volunteers, positive expectation doubled the analgesic benefit of remifentanil and negative expectation abolished it [@bingel2011].

## Evidence

A meta-analysis of 96 randomised trials in chronic non-cancer pain found high-quality evidence that opioids reduce pain and improve physical function compared with placebo, but by less than the minimally important difference on average; compared with NSAIDs, tricyclic antidepressants and anticonvulsants, benefits for pain and function appeared similar, on low- to moderate-quality evidence [@busse2018]. In a pragmatic 12-month trial in chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis pain, opioids were not better than non-opioid medicines for function, pain was slightly worse, and adverse symptoms were more common [@krebs2018]. In neuropathic pain, opioids including tramadol have an NNT of about 6 but low-certainty evidence [@soliman2025]; the Cochrane review of tramadol rated the evidence low or very low [@duehmke2017].

## In practice

Guidelines converge on keeping opioids out of first-line care for chronic pain: NICE advises against starting them for chronic primary pain, chronic low back pain, chronic sciatica and osteoarthritis [@nice2021; @nice2016; @nice2022], the CDC prefers non-opioid therapies for subacute and chronic pain [@dowell2022], and NeuPSIG places all opioids, including tramadol, third line for neuropathic pain [@soliman2025]. People already taking opioids need a shared plan, because stopping can cause withdrawal problems [@nice2021].
