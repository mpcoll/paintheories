## Overview

Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques aim to stimulate the brain through the scalp and so alter its activity [@oconnell2018]. rTMS was developed a few years after invasive epidural motor cortex stimulation, and the motor cortex remains its main studied and validated target for pain [@lefaucheur2016].

## Evidence

The two main syntheses differ in scope. The 2025 NeuPSIG meta-analysis, restricted to double-blind trials in neuropathic pain with at least 3 weeks of treatment or follow-up, estimated that about four people need treatment for one more to respond than with sham, but with low certainty and a confidence interval reaching an NNT of 28 [@soliman2025]. The 2018 Cochrane review of non-invasive brain stimulation across chronic pain found a small short-term effect of rTMS over sham overall and for single sessions of high-frequency motor cortex stimulation, no evidence of an effect for multiple sessions (very low-quality evidence), no evidence that low-frequency or prefrontal stimulation reduces pain, and no evidence of improvement in disability [@oconnell2018].

## In practice

NeuPSIG places rTMS third line, after first-line antidepressants and α2δ-ligands and second-line topical treatments [@soliman2025]. Relief in neuropathic pain has been reported with repeated daily sessions over weeks, and a 2016 review noted that targeting still needs to improve to reduce the proportion of non-responders [@lefaucheur2016].
