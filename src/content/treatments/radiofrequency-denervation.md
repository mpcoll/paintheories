## Overview

Radiofrequency denervation is used most often for low back pain suspected to arise from the facet joints, sacroiliac joints or intervertebral discs; trials select people who responded to a diagnostic block or discography [@maas2015]. Nearly every aspect of patient selection and technique remains debated, from the number of diagnostic blocks to the cut-off for a positive block and the size of the lesion [@cohen2020].

## Evidence

A 2015 Cochrane review of 23 trials (1309 participants) found no high-quality evidence that radiofrequency denervation relieves chronic low back pain. For suspected facet joint pain, moderate-quality evidence showed a short-term benefit over placebo; for disc pain the results were conflicting, and for sacroiliac joint pain low-quality evidence showed no short-term difference [@maas2015]. The three pragmatic MINT trials in the Netherlands (681 participants) added radiofrequency denervation to a standardised exercise programme and found either no improvement or no clinically important improvement over exercise alone at 3 months [@juch2017]. MINT did not use a sham procedure, and it was widely criticised for inserting the electrodes perpendicular to the target nerves; a consensus group notes that differences between trials preclude firm conclusions about such technical factors [@cohen2020].

## In practice

NICE suggests considering referral when non-surgical treatment has failed and a medial branch block has given a positive response [@nice2016]. A multispecialty consensus group concluded that lumbar medial branch ablation may benefit well-selected people, and that stricter selection criteria are likely to improve outcomes at the expense of more false negatives [@cohen2020].
