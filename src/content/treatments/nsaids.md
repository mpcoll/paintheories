## Overview

NSAIDs reduce the prostaglandins that sensitize nociceptors in inflamed tissue [@basbaum2009]. They come as tablets and as gels or creams applied over the painful joint.

## Evidence

In chronic low back pain, NSAIDs reduced pain more than placebo, but by only about 3 points on a 0 to 100 scale, and the evidence was of low quality; effects shrank when only trials at low risk of bias were included [@enthoven2016]. In knee and hip osteoarthritis, a network meta-analysis of large trials found that all NSAID preparations improved pain, with diclofenac 150 mg/day and etoricoxib 60 mg/day most likely to be the most effective [@dacosta2017]. Topical diclofenac and ketoprofen give good pain relief beyond carrier to a minority of people with knee osteoarthritis; the carrier alone produces a large response, and there is no evidence for topical NSAIDs in other chronic pain conditions [@derry2016].

## In practice

NICE recommends topical NSAIDs first for knee osteoarthritis and oral NSAIDs, with gastroprotection, when topical treatment is not enough [@nice2022]; it suggests considering oral NSAIDs for low back pain [@nice2016] but advises against starting them for chronic primary pain [@nice2021]. For sciatica, NICE asks prescribers to be aware of the risk of harms and limited evidence of benefit [@nice2016]. The size of gastrointestinal and vascular risks differs between drugs and doses and can be predicted from a person's baseline risk [@cnt2013].
