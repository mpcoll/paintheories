## Overview

Interdisciplinary programmes grew from the biopsychosocial model of chronic pain [@gatchel2007]. NICE defines a pain management programme as any intervention with two or more components, including a physical and a psychological component, delivered by trained people with some coordination between the components [@nice2021].

## Evidence

The Cochrane review of multidisciplinary biopsychosocial rehabilitation for chronic low back pain included 41 trials and 6858 participants, who had a mean pain duration of more than a year and had often failed previous treatment. Compared with usual care, programmes reduced pain and disability slightly (moderate-quality evidence) but did not improve the odds of being at work. Compared with physical treatments, the evidence was of low quality and heterogeneous: disability improved, the confidence interval for pain only just included no effect, and the odds of being at work one year later were higher (odds ratio 1.87, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.53, moderate quality) [@kamper2015].

## In practice

Guidelines diverge. The ACP lists multidisciplinary rehabilitation among first-choice treatments for chronic low back pain [@qaseem2017], and EULAR suggests a multimodal rehabilitation programme for people with fibromyalgia and severe disability [@macfarlane2017]. NICE made no recommendation for chronic primary pain: for mixed chronic pain it found small benefits in quality of life, but it could not tell which content or format of programme works, and programmes did not show the added benefit that might be expected from combining interventions [@nice2021]. NICE also removed its low back pain recommendation on combined physical and psychological programmes in July 2026 [@nice2016].
