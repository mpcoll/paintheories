## Overview

PRT was tested in adults with primary chronic back pain, for which no peripheral cause can be identified. Participants had one telehealth session with a physician and eight psychological sessions over 4 weeks, aimed at reconceptualising their pain as due to non-dangerous brain activity [@ashar2022].

## Evidence

In the trial, 151 adults from the community with low to moderate pain (mean 4.1 of 10) and a mean pain duration of 10 years were randomized to PRT, an open-label placebo injection or usual care. Pain fell to a mean of 1.18 with PRT, against 2.84 with placebo and 3.13 with usual care, and the differences were maintained at 1 year [@ashar2022]. At 5 years, 75% of participants returned data; PRT still had lower pain intensity than both control groups, and improvements in pain interference, depression, anger, fear of movement and attributions of pain to mind or brain processes, but no significant effect on sleep, anxiety, pain catastrophizing or perceived control of pain [@ashar2025].

## Open questions

- All of this evidence comes from a single randomized trial, run in a university research setting with a community sample whose pain was of low to moderate severity; it needs independent replication, including in clinical populations with more severe pain [@ashar2022].
- Participants knew which treatment they received, as in any trial of a psychological therapy. Several authors reported financial interests related to the treatment, such as fees for running PRT trainings [@ashar2025].
- Which of the cognitive, somatic and exposure-based components carries the effect is not known.
