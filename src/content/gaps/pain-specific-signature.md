title: Is there brain activity specific to pain?
order: 2
summary: Distributed brain responses to noxious stimuli were long taken as a "pain matrix". Salience accounts argue that most of this activity reflects detecting salient events in any modality. Whether any pattern is specific to pain is central to neural theories of pain and to the use of brain measures as pain biomarkers.
questions: [nature]
predictions:
  - theory: salience-detection-account
    prediction: Non-nociceptive stimuli matched for salience should evoke responses indistinguishable from those to nociceptive stimuli, and these responses should be present in people who cannot feel pain.
  - theory: neuromatrix-theory
    prediction: A distributed pattern should distinguish pain from equally salient non-painful events, and should track reported pain even when stimulus intensity and salience are held constant.
  - theory: three-dimensional-model
    prediction: Sensory and affective dimensions of pain should map onto partly separable systems, each of which should show pain-specific responses when salience is matched.
  - theory: dynamic-pain-connectome
    prediction: Pain should be represented in the dynamics and interactions of networks rather than in any single region, so specificity should appear at the level of network dynamics and not regional activation.
test:
  design: Within-subject fMRI and intracranial recordings with nociceptive, tactile, auditory and visual stimuli individually matched on rated salience, arousal and unexpectedness, analysed with multivariate decoding, and repeated in people with congenital insensitivity to pain.
  decisive: Above-chance discrimination of pain from salience-matched non-painful stimuli, generalising across individuals, would favour a pain-specific signature. Discrimination that disappears after salience matching, or similar patterns in pain-insensitive people, would favour the salience account.

## Current evidence

Most of the fMRI and EEG response to brief nociceptive stimuli is also elicited by equally salient non-nociceptive stimuli and scales with salience [@mouraux2011; @iannetti2008]. Intracranial insular recordings show multimodal responses [@liberati2016], and people with congenital insensitivity to pain show similar "pain matrix" responses [@salomons2016]. On the other hand, a multivariate fMRI signature discriminates painful heat from warmth, anticipation and recall and is reduced by opioids [@wager2013], although it has not been tested against salience-matched non-nociceptive stimuli.
