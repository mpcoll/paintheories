title: What drives the transition from acute to chronic pain?
order: 1
summary: Most theories of chronic pain name a different driver of chronification — spinal amplification, corticolimbic predisposition, fear and avoidance, over-generalised learning, immune signalling or miscalibrated beliefs about injury. They are rarely tested against each other in the same people, so their relative contributions and causal order remain unknown.
questions: [chronicity, disability]
predictions:
  - theory: central-sensitization
    prediction: Early, widespread increases in temporal summation and secondary hyperalgesia after injury should precede and predict persistent pain, independently of psychological variables.
  - theory: corticolimbic-learning-theory
    prediction: Corticostriatal connectivity and limbic anatomy measured at or before onset should predict persistence better than nociceptive or psychological measures, and should not depend on the amount of early pain.
  - theory: fear-avoidance-model
    prediction: Early catastrophizing and pain-related fear should predict disability at follow-up, with increases in avoidance and disuse mediating the effect.
  - theory: imprecision-hypothesis
    prediction: People who generalise protective responses more broadly (flatter generalisation gradients) in a learning task soon after onset should be more likely to develop persistent pain.
  - theory: neuroimmune-theory
    prediction: Early inflammatory and glial markers should predict persistence, and modulating them early should change the course of pain.
  - theory: homeostatic-injury-model
    prediction: Persistence should follow protective behaviour that continues after tissue recovery, reflecting beliefs about the state of the injury that fail to update.
test:
  design: A prospective cohort recruited at the onset of acute pain (e.g. acute low back pain or before and after surgery), measuring all candidate predictors at baseline and repeatedly over the first three months — quantitative sensory testing, resting-state and structural MRI, catastrophizing and fear, a generalisation learning task, inflammatory markers and protective behaviour — with pre-registered model comparison of outcomes at 6–12 months.
  decisive: Which predictors add unique variance when all are in the same model, and whether changes in each proposed mediator precede (rather than follow) the change in pain. A single dominant predictor would favour that theory; additive, independent contributions would favour integrative accounts.
references: [baliki2012, vachonpresseau2016, picavet2002, pincus2002, wertli2014, harvie2017, woolf2011, mahajan2026]

## Current evidence

Prospective studies support several candidates, but mostly in isolation. Corticostriatal connectivity predicted persistence of subacute back pain [@baliki2012], and corticolimbic anatomy accounted for much of the variance in persistence over three years [@vachonpresseau2016]. Catastrophizing and kinesiophobia predicted chronic, disabling back pain in a population cohort [@picavet2002], whereas systematic reviews find more consistent evidence for distress and depressive mood than for catastrophizing [@pincus2002], and fear-avoidance beliefs predict outcome mainly in the subacute phase [@wertli2014]. Differences in conditioning and generalisation have been found in people who already have chronic pain, so whether they precede it is unknown [@harvie2017].
