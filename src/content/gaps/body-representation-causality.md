title: Are altered body representations a cause or a consequence of pain?
order: 5
summary: Several theories hold that distorted body maps or sensorimotor conflict generate or maintain pain, and treatments such as mirror therapy and graded motor imagery follow from this. But the evidence is mostly correlational and partly contradictory, and the direction of causation is unclear.
questions: [chronicity, nature]
predictions:
  - theory: maladaptive-cortical-plasticity
    prediction: The degree of reorganisation of somatosensory maps should predict pain, and reversing the reorganisation should reduce pain.
  - theory: cortical-body-matrix
    prediction: Manipulating where or how the body is perceived, without changing input, should change pain and autonomic symptoms in the affected limb.
  - theory: sensorimotor-incongruence
    prediction: Experimentally induced conflict between motor intention and sensory feedback should increase pain and abnormal sensations, more in patients than in controls.
  - theory: protective-output-account
    prediction: Visual or contextual cues signalling threat to a body part should change pain independently of body-map distortion.
test:
  design: Longitudinal imaging of body representations before and after limb amputation or injury, from before pain onset, combined with randomised, sham-controlled manipulations of body representation (visual distortion, prism adaptation, graded motor imagery) and mediation analysis.
  decisive: Whether changes in representation precede pain and mediate treatment effects. Reorganisation that follows rather than precedes pain, or treatment effects without representational change, would count against a causal role.

## Current evidence

Phantom pain correlated with somatosensory reorganisation in an early study [@flor1995], but later work associated more phantom pain with better-preserved representations of the missing hand [@makin2013; @makin2015]. In CRPS, temperature changes followed the perceived rather than actual position of the hand [@moseley2013]. Sensorimotor conflict induced abnormal sensations in healthy volunteers [@mccabe2005], but in whiplash symptoms did not differ between congruent and incongruent conditions [@daenen2012]. Bogus visual feedback changed the onset of movement-evoked neck pain [@harvie2015].
